Topic 7: Order of Operations

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic evaluates numerical and variable expressions in an agreed order so that a single expression has a consistent value.

Key definitions

Exponent: the number of times a base is used as a factor. Base: the quantity being raised to a power. Order of operations: grouping symbols, exponents, multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right. Substitution: replacing a variable with a given value.

Use the exponent pattern to determine the value of 3 raised to the zero power.

Begin with

\[3^{3} = 27.\]

The meaning of a positive exponent is easy to see through repeated multiplication. To understand exponents of zero and negative exponents, it helps to look at the pattern created as we decrease the exponent one step at a time.

Reducing the exponent from 3 to 2 removes one factor of 3. Equivalently, we divide the previous value by 3:

\[27 \div 3 = 9.\]

Therefore,

\[3^{2} = 9.\]

Reducing the exponent again means dividing by 3 again:

\[9 \div 3 = 3.\]

Therefore,

\[3^{1} = 3.\]

The same pattern must continue when the exponent decreases from 1 to 0:

\[3 \div 3 = 1.\]

Therefore,

\[3^{0} = 1.\]

It can be tempting to think that an exponent of zero should make the answer zero. However, the exponent is not being multiplied by the base. The exponent tells us how many copies of the base are being multiplied. More importantly, the sequence of values must continue consistently: every time the exponent drops by one, the value is divided by the base.

For the nonzero bases used in this topic,

\[a^{0} = 1.\]

For example,

\[2^{0} = 1,\]

\[( - 4)^{0} = 1,\]

and

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{0} = 1.\]

An exponent is a compact way to represent repeated multiplication. In this topic, we will move among three closely related forms: the exponent form, the expansion, and the value. The exponent form is the original expression written with an exponent. The expansion shows the repeated multiplication represented by that exponent. The value is the number obtained after carrying out the multiplication.

For example,

\[3^{3}\]

is in exponent form. Its expansion is

\[3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3\]

and its value is

\[27.\]

The goal is not merely to memorize isolated rules. We want to understand what the notation says and how the values fit together as the exponent changes.

What the base and exponent tell us

In an expression such as

\[3^{4},\]

the base is 3 and the exponent is 4. The base is the factor being repeated. The exponent tells us how many copies of the base appear in the repeated multiplication.

Therefore,

\[3^{4}\]

expands to

\[3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3.\]

Its value is

\[81.\]

The exponent is not another number that we multiply by the base. For example,

\[3^{2}\]

does not mean

\[3 \cdot 2.\]

It means that two copies of 3 are multiplied:

\[3 \cdot 3.\]

Thus,

\[3^{2} = 9.\]

The same interpretation gives us

\[3^{1} = 3.\]

There is only one copy of the base, so no additional multiplication is needed.

Negative exponents

We can continue the same pattern below zero. Starting with

\[3^{0} = 1,\]

we decrease the exponent by one and divide the value by 3:

\[1 \div 3 = \frac{1}{3}.\]

Therefore,

\[3^{- 1} = \frac{1}{3}.\]

Decrease the exponent again and divide by 3 again:

\[\frac{1}{3} \div 3 = \frac{1}{3 \cdot 3}.\]

Therefore,

\[3^{- 2} = \frac{1}{3 \cdot 3}.\]

After multiplying the denominator,

\[3^{- 2} = \frac{1}{9}.\]

One more step gives

\[3^{- 3} = \frac{1}{3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3}.\]

Therefore,

\[3^{- 3} = \frac{1}{27}.\]

A negative exponent does not automatically make the value negative. Instead, it tells us to take the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. In general,

\[a^{- n} = \frac{1}{a^{n}}.\]

For example,

\[5^{- 2} = \frac{1}{5^{2}}.\]

Expanding the denominator gives

\[5^{- 2} = \frac{1}{5 \cdot 5}.\]

Therefore,

\[5^{- 2} = \frac{1}{25}.\]

The negative sign on the exponent changes where the repeated factors appear. Positive exponents place the factors in the numerator. Negative exponents place the factors in the denominator.

Identify the base and evaluate the power.

\[(-4)^2\]

Parentheses make the negative sign part of the base. The base is

\[- 4.\]

The exponent applies to the entire base inside the parentheses. Therefore, the expansion is

\[( - 4)( - 4).\]

The value is

\[16.\]

For the third power,

\[( - 4)^{3}\]

expands to

\[( - 4)( - 4)( - 4).\]

The first two negative factors multiply to a positive number, and the remaining negative factor makes the final answer negative. Thus,

\[( - 4)^{3} = - 64.\]

This creates a useful pattern. A negative base raised to an even positive exponent has a positive value because the negative factors occur in pairs. A negative base raised to an odd positive exponent has a negative value because one negative factor remains unpaired.

For example,

\[( - 4)^{1} = - 4,\]

\[( - 4)^{2} = 16,\]

and

\[( - 4)^{3} = - 64.\]

The exponent-zero pattern still applies:

\[( - 4)^{0} = 1.\]

Negative exponents produce reciprocals in the same way as before. For example,

\[( - 4)^{- 1} = \frac{1}{( - 4)^{1}}.\]

Therefore,

\[( - 4)^{- 1} = - \frac{1}{4}.\]

Similarly,

\[( - 4)^{- 2} = \frac{1}{( - 4)^{2}}.\]

Expanding the denominator gives

\[( - 4)^{- 2} = \frac{1}{( - 4)( - 4)}.\]

Therefore,

\[( - 4)^{- 2} = \frac{1}{16}.\]

For the third negative power,

\[( - 4)^{- 3} = \frac{1}{( - 4)^{3}}.\]

Therefore,

\[( - 4)^{- 3} = - \frac{1}{64}.\]

The even-or-odd pattern still matters. A negative exponent changes the number to a reciprocal, but it does not erase the sign pattern created by the negative base.

Compare and contrast these two expressions: evaluate each and explain how the parentheses affect the result.

\[(-4)^2\]

\[-4^2\]

Now compare

\[( - 4)^{2}\]

with

\[- 4^{2}.\]

These expressions look similar, but they do not mean the same thing.

In

\[( - 4)^{2},\]

the parentheses make

\[- 4\]

the base. The exponent applies to both the 4 and its negative sign:

\[( - 4)^{2} = ( - 4)( - 4) = 16.\]

In

\[- 4^{2},\]

the base of the power is only 4. The exponent applies to 4 first, and the negative sign remains outside the power:

\[- 4^{2} = - \left( 4^{2} \right).\]

Expanding the power gives

\[- 4^{2} = - (4 \cdot 4).\]

Therefore,

\[- 4^{2} = - 16.\]

The same distinction appears with an exponent of zero. Since the exponent applies only to 4,

\[- 4^{0} = - \left( 4^{0} \right).\]

Because

\[4^{0} = 1,\]

we have

\[- 4^{0} = - 1.\]

Negative exponents work similarly. In

\[- 4^{- 2},\]

the exponent applies only to 4:

\[- 4^{- 2} = - \left( 4^{- 2} \right).\]

Replace the negative exponent with a reciprocal:

\[- 4^{- 2} = - \frac{1}{4^{2}}.\]

Expand the denominator:

\[- 4^{- 2} = - \frac{1}{4 \cdot 4}.\]

Therefore,

\[- 4^{- 2} = - \frac{1}{16}.\]

Compare and contrast these two expressions: evaluate each and explain how the parentheses affect the result.

\[(-4)^{-2}\]

\[-4^{-2}\]

\[(-4)^{-2}=\frac{1}{(-4)^2}=\frac{1}{16}.\]

For the expression without parentheses,

\[- 4^{- 2} = - \frac{1}{16}.\]

When the negative sign is inside parentheses, it is part of the base and is repeated. When the negative sign is outside the power, it is not repeated. The exponent is evaluated first, and the outside negative sign is applied afterward.

Exponents with fractional bases

The same ideas apply when the base is a fraction. Consider

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{2}.\]

The base is the entire fraction. The exponent tells us to use two copies of that fraction as factors:

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)\left( \frac{5}{4} \right).\]

Multiplying the numerators and denominators gives

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{2} = \frac{25}{16}.\]

Similarly,

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{3}\]

expands to

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)\left( \frac{5}{4} \right).\]

Therefore,

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{3} = \frac{125}{64}.\]

The zero power is still 1:

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{0} = 1.\]

For a negative exponent, take the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. Begin with

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 1} = \frac{1}{\frac{5}{4}}.\]

Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal:

\[1 \div \frac{5}{4} = 1 \cdot \frac{4}{5}.\]

Therefore,

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 1} = \frac{4}{5}.\]

For the second negative power,

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 2} = \left( \frac{4}{5} \right)^{2}.\]

Expanding gives

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 2} = \left( \frac{4}{5} \right)\left( \frac{4}{5} \right).\]

Therefore,

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 2} = \frac{16}{25}.\]

For the third negative power,

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 3} = \left( \frac{4}{5} \right)^{3}.\]

Expanding gives

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 3} = \left( \frac{4}{5} \right)\left( \frac{4}{5} \right)\left( \frac{4}{5} \right).\]

Therefore,

\[\left( \frac{5}{4} \right)^{- 3} = \frac{64}{125}.\]

A useful way to evaluate a fraction raised to a negative exponent is to reverse the fraction and then use the corresponding positive exponent:

\[\left( \frac{a}{b} \right)^{- n} = \left( \frac{b}{a} \right)^{n}.\]

This is the same reciprocal rule we used for whole-number bases. The notation looks different only because the base is already a fraction.

Bringing the ideas together

When evaluating an exponent, first identify exactly what the base is. Parentheses may make a negative number or a fraction into one complete base. Then examine the exponent.

A positive exponent tells how many copies of the base are multiplied.

An exponent of zero gives a value of 1 for the nonzero bases used here.

A negative exponent tells us to take the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power.

Finally, pay close attention to whether a negative sign is inside or outside parentheses. The expressions

\[( - 3)^{2}\]

and

\[- 3^{2}\]

are different because they have different bases. In the first expression, the base is

\[- 3.\]

In the second expression, the base is

\[3,\]

with a negative sign outside the power.

Evaluate the expression.

\(2 + 3 \cdot 4\)

One possible first move would be to add two and three. That would produce the following calculation.

\(2 + 3 \cdot 4 = 5 \cdot 4 = 20\)

Another possible first move would be to multiply three and four. That produces a different calculation.

\(2 + 3 \cdot 4 = 2 + 12 = 14\)

The original expression cannot be allowed to have both values. We therefore use a convention: an arbitrary rule that everyone agrees to follow. In this convention, multiplication takes precedence over addition. The multiplication must be evaluated before the addition, so the value of the expression is fourteen. The step that changes two plus three into five is not a valid first step because the three is also involved in a multiplication, and that multiplication has higher precedence.

A numerical expression can contain several operations at the same time. The symbols tell us which operations are present, but we also need an agreement about which operation takes precedence. Without that agreement, two people could read the same expression and obtain different values.

The convention

The order of operations is a convention regarding which operations take precedence over others when evaluating a numerical or algebraic expression. Exponents take precedence over multiplication and division, and multiplication and division take precedence over addition and subtraction.

Parentheses, brackets, and fraction bars are grouping symbols. They tell us which pieces form a complete group. Simplify within a group before using that group in an operation outside it. Within every group, apply the same precedence rules: exponents first, then multiplication and division, and then addition and subtraction.

Multiplication and division have the same level of precedence. Addition and subtraction also have the same level of precedence. When two operations at the same level compete for the same number, work from left to right.

A useful way to check a proposed step is to focus on the operation between two adjacent numbers. Before evaluating that operation, inspect the neighboring operations involving either of those numbers. If a neighboring operation has higher precedence, the proposed step must wait. This check is often more reliable than trying to recite a memorized slogan while looking at the whole expression at once.

Evaluate.

\(2 + 3 \cdot 4 + 5 \cdot 2^{3} + 6 + 7\)

The addition of two and three is not available yet because the three is part of the product three times four. The exponent on two is also waiting inside a product, but the exponent itself has the highest precedence and may be evaluated. The product three times four may be evaluated, and the final six plus seven may also be evaluated because neither number is involved in a higher-precedence operation. These independent computations can be carried out in the same line.

= \(2 + 12 + 5 \cdot 8 + 13\)

Now the product five times eight must be evaluated before it is combined with the neighboring additions. The first two terms may also be added because twelve is no longer involved in a multiplication.

= \(14 + 40 + 13\)

Only addition remains, so the expression can be evaluated to a single number.

= \(67\)

The steps do not have to be performed in exactly this combination. For example, the exponent could be evaluated on a line by itself. What matters is that every individual change is valid and that every displayed expression has the same value as the original.

Evaluate.

\(2 + 3 + 4 \cdot 5^{2} + 6 + 7 \cdot 2\)

Begin with the exponent. The product seven times two does not depend on that exponent, so it may also be evaluated at the same time.

= \(2 + 3 + 4 \cdot 25 + 6 + 14\)

The twenty-five is part of the product four times twenty-five. That multiplication must be completed before the surrounding additions.

= \(2 + 3 + 100 + 6 + 14\)

All of the remaining operations are additions.

= \(125\)

Simplify using the order of operations.

\(1 \cdot 2 + 2(14 - 5) + 7\)

The parentheses make fourteen minus five a complete group. The two immediately beside the parentheses means multiplication, even though no multiplication dot is written. First evaluate inside the parentheses. The separate product one times two can also be evaluated.

= \(2 + 2(9) + 7\)

The notation two followed by parentheses still means two times nine. Writing the multiplication dot makes that operation explicit without changing the value.

= \(2 + 2 \cdot 9 + 7\)

Evaluate the multiplication before the additions.

= \(2 + 18 + 7\)

= \(27\)

Simplify the expression using the order of operations.

\(\frac{5(3 + 6) + 3(4 - 3)}{3(9 - 2) - 3^{2}}\)

The fraction bar groups the entire numerator and the entire denominator. Simplify each of those expressions before carrying out the division represented by the fraction bar. The three parenthetical calculations may be completed first.

\(\frac{5(9) + 3(1)}{3(7) - 3^{2}}\)

In the denominator, the exponent belongs to the three only. The subtraction sign in front of the power is not part of the base. Exponentiation therefore happens before the subtraction.

\(- 3^{2} = - 3 \cdot 3 = - 9\)

This is different from squaring a negative number written inside parentheses. Parentheses would make the negative sign part of the base.

\({( - 3)}^{2} = 9\)

Now evaluate the products in the numerator and denominator.

= \(\frac{45 + 3}{21 - 9}\)

Complete the additions and subtraction within the fraction.

= \(\frac{48}{12}\)

Finally, divide forty-eight by twelve.

= \(4\)

Find the numerical value if x = 4 and y = 5.

\(8x^{2} + 2y\)

First replace each variable with its given value. Parentheses make it clear that four is replacing x and five is replacing y. After the substitution, the expression is numerical, and the usual order of operations applies.

\(8 \cdot {(4)}^{2} + 2 \cdot (5)\)

Evaluate the exponent before either multiplication. The second product does not depend on the exponent, so it may be evaluated on the same line.

= \(8 \cdot 16 + 10\)

Complete the remaining multiplication.

= \(128 + 10\)

Then add.

= \(138\)

Evaluate 3p² for p = −2.

\(3p^{2}\)

Replace p with negative two. The parentheses are important because the entire negative number is the base of the exponent. The exponent belongs to p, so after the substitution it belongs to the negative two inside the parentheses. The coefficient three is not part of the base and is not squared.

\(3 \cdot {( - 2)}^{2}\)

Evaluate the exponent first.

= \(3 \cdot 4\)

Then multiply.

= \(12\)

Checking each line

Order of operations is not merely a command to finish a problem in one rigid-looking sequence. At some stages, more than one independent operation is available. At other stages, an operation that looks easy must wait because one of its numbers is still involved in a higher-precedence operation. For every proposed change, identify the two numbers being combined, check the neighboring operations, and make sure the new line has exactly the same value as the previous line.
