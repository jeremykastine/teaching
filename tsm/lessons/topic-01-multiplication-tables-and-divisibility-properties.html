Topic 1: Division and Divisibility

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This course assumes that you can perform basic addition, subtraction, and multiplication. Here we develop division: how to write it, how successive divisions can be combined or separated, and how that connects to divisibility. Then we use multiplication-table patterns to investigate divisibility by 2, 5, 10, 3, and 9, and examine why those same tests do not work for 7.

Key definitions

Quotient: The result of division. Divisor: The number by which we divide; it must be nonzero.

Successive divisions: Dividing a result again. Without parentheses that change the grouping, successive multiplication and division operations are evaluated from left to right.

Multiple: A number obtained by multiplying a given whole number by a whole number.

Divisible: A whole number is divisible by a positive whole number when the division has remainder 0.

Factor: A whole number that divides another whole number evenly.

Divisibility property: A shortcut for deciding whether a number is divisible by another number without carrying out the division.

Division reverses multiplication

Dividing a quantity into equal groups asks how much belongs in each group. Multiplication checks the result: the quotient multiplied by the divisor must give the original quantity. Division by zero is undefined. In this topic our divisors are positive whole numbers.

Use \(6\cdot9=54\) to state the two related division facts.

The product tells us that 54 can be shared into six groups of nine or nine groups of six. Thus \(54\div6=9\) and \(54\div9=6\). Each quotient multiplied by its divisor returns 54. This connection supports divisibility, fraction reduction, factoring, and equation solving.

Ways to write division

The division symbol \(\div\), a slash, and a fraction bar can all indicate division. The fraction bar groups the entire numerator and the entire denominator; use parentheses when a slash would otherwise leave the grouping unclear. Fraction notation is also useful for keeping an exact answer when a quotient is not a whole number.

Rewrite \(12\div3\) using a slash and a fraction bar. Evaluate and explain why the forms agree.

Sharing 12 into three equal parts gives 4 in each part. Each notation describes that same division.

\[12\div3=12/3=\frac{12}{3}=4\]

The multiplication check is \(4\cdot3=12\). All three forms give the same quotient.

Rewrite \(1\div3\) using a fraction bar. Give the exact answer.

One whole shared into three equal parts gives one third. A fraction bar records this division exactly.

\[1\div3=\frac13\]

The exact answer is \(\frac13\); no decimal approximation is needed.

Write \(\frac{8+4}{2+1}\) using a slash and using \(\div\), with parentheses to preserve the grouping. Evaluate.

The fraction bar divides the entire numerator by the entire denominator. Parentheses preserve those two groups in horizontal notation.

\[\frac{8+4}{2+1}=(8+4)/(2+1)\]

\[(8+4)\div(2+1)=12\div3=4\]

Combining and separating successive divisions

Dividing by one positive whole number and then another is equivalent to dividing once by their product. Conversely, a single division by a product can be separated into successive divisions by its factors. Read an unparenthesized string of divisions from left to right; dividing by a quotient in parentheses is a different operation.

This connects division to divisibility: if a whole number is divisible by a product, dividing it by one factor gives a whole-number quotient that is divisible by the other factor. Successive divisions by those factors therefore come out evenly.

Evaluate \(24\div3\div4\) from left to right. Combine the successive divisions into one division, and explain why the quotient stays the same.

First divide 24 by 3, then divide the result by 4.

\[24\div3\div4=8\div4=2\]

Splitting each of three equal groups into four gives twelve equal groups altogether. Multiply the divisors to combine the divisions.

\[24\div3\div4=24\div(3\cdot4)=24\div12=2\]

Separate \(42\div6\) into division by 2 followed by division by 3. Evaluate, and explain how this relates to divisibility by 6.

Since \(6=2\cdot3\), division by 6 can be separated into those two successive divisions.

\[42\div6=42\div2\div3=21\div3=7\]

Forty-two is divisible by 6. Dividing it by 2 gives 21, which is divisible by 3. Both divisions come out evenly, and their combined effect is division by 6.

Combine \(1\div3\div3\div3\) into one division. Give the exact answer as a fraction.

Three successive divisions by 3 combine into division by the product of the three divisors.

\[1\div3\div3\div3=1\div(3\cdot3\cdot3)=1\div27=\frac1{27}\]

The fraction bar expresses the final division exactly; no decimal approximation is needed.

Separate \(60\div12\) into division by 3 followed by division by 4. Evaluate and explain why this preserves the quotient.

Because \(12=3\cdot4\), division by 12 can be separated into those two successive divisions.

\[60\div12=60\div3\div4=20\div4=5\]

We could also divide by 4 and then by 3. Both orders divide by the same product.

Multiplication facts for divisibility

Multiplication describes equal groups. Reversing the order of two factors does not change their product. Division reverses multiplication, so one multiplication fact supplies two related division facts. Practice these facts through \(12\cdot12\), especially those that are not yet automatic.

Use the multiplication fact \(7\cdot8=56\) to describe two ways of arranging equal groups and to state the reversed multiplication fact.

Multiplication facts describe equal groups. For example, \(7\cdot8=56\) means seven groups of eight and also eight groups of seven. The commutative property lets one fact support the reversed fact. Changing the order of the factors does not change the product.

\[7\cdot8=8\cdot7=56\]

Compare and contrast the following two problems. Use known multiplication facts to find \(6\cdot8\) from \(5\cdot8=40\), and to find \(9\cdot7\) from \(10\cdot7=70\).

Use known facts to build less familiar ones. Since \(5\cdot8=40\), one more group of eight gives \(6\cdot8=48\). Since \(10\cdot7=70\), subtract one group of seven to get \(9\cdot7=63\).

\[6\cdot8=5\cdot8+8=40+8=48\]

\[9\cdot7=10\cdot7-7=70-7=63\]

Both problems use a familiar multiplication fact to find a nearby fact. The first adds one group; the second subtracts one group.

Divisibility

Divisibility is a yes-or-no question about a whole number. When we ask whether a number is divisible by another number, we are asking whether it can be separated into equal groups with nothing left over. If nothing is left over, the answer is yes. If there is a remainder, the answer is no.

A divisibility property lets us answer that question without actually carrying out the division. Some properties depend only on the final digit. Others depend on the sum of the digits. These properties tell us whether division would come out evenly; they do not require us to find the quotient.

Compare and contrast the following two problems. Determine whether 24 is divisible by 2 and whether 25 is divisible by 2.

Twenty-four is divisible by two because it can be written as \(2\cdot12\). That is, twenty-four contains exactly twelve groups of two, with nothing left over.

Twenty-five is not divisible by two. Twelve groups of two account for twenty-four, leaving one extra. In other words, dividing twenty-five by two would produce a remainder of one.

Final-digit patterns

Compare and contrast counting by 2, 5, and 10. List the first ten positive multiples of each number. Identify the repeating final digits and explain the corresponding divisibility tests.

The first ten positive multiples of 2 are \(2,\allowbreak4,\allowbreak6,\allowbreak8,\allowbreak10,\allowbreak12,\allowbreak14,\allowbreak16,\allowbreak18,\allowbreak20\). Their final digits cycle through 2, 4, 6, 8, and 0, so divisibility by 2 is determined by an even final digit.

For 5, the first ten multiples are \(5,\allowbreak10,\allowbreak15,\allowbreak20,\allowbreak25,\allowbreak30,\allowbreak35,\allowbreak40,\allowbreak45,\allowbreak50\). Their final digits alternate between 5 and 0, giving the divisibility test for 5.

For 10, the first ten multiples are \(10,\allowbreak20,\allowbreak30,\allowbreak40,\allowbreak50,\allowbreak60,\allowbreak70,\allowbreak80,\allowbreak90,\allowbreak100\). Every final digit is 0. All three tests use the final digit, but the allowed final digits differ.

Tests using the final digit

A whole number is divisible by 2 when its final digit is even, by 5 when its final digit is 0 or 5, and by 10 when its final digit is 0.

Without dividing, determine whether 872 is divisible by 2.

The final digit is 2. Since every number ending in 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 is divisible by 2, 872 is divisible by 2.

Without dividing, determine whether 4187 is divisible by 5.

The final digit is 7. Multiples of 5 end in 0 or 5, so 4187 is not divisible by 5.

Without dividing, determine whether 65290 is divisible by 10.

The final digit is 0. Every multiple of 10 ends in 0, so 65290 is divisible by 10.

Without dividing, determine whether 513927 is divisible by 2.

The final digit is 7. A number ending in 7 is not divisible by 2, so 513927 is not divisible by 2.

Without dividing, determine whether 7835 is divisible by 5.

The final digit is 5. Multiples of 5 end in 0 or 5, so 7835 is divisible by 5.

Without dividing, determine whether 204316 is divisible by 10.

The final digit is 6. Multiples of 10 end in 0, so 204316 is not divisible by 10.

Determine whether 233,557 is divisible by 2.

The final digit is seven. Seven is odd, so the number is not divisible by two.

Determine whether 418,980 is divisible by 5.

The final digit is zero, so the number is divisible by five. The fact that the number also happens to be divisible by other numbers is not needed here; the question only asks about divisibility by five.

Determine whether 665,926 is divisible by 10.

The final digit is six rather than zero. The number is therefore not divisible by ten.

Digit-sum patterns

Compare and contrast divisibility by 3 and 9. List the first nine positive multiples of each number and examine the digit sums of the two-digit multiples. Also test 123, 372, and 1248 for divisibility by 3, and 117, 459, 1206, and 8019 for divisibility by 9. Explain the digit-sum tests.

The first nine positive multiples of 3 are \(3,\allowbreak6,\allowbreak9,\allowbreak12,\allowbreak15,\allowbreak18,\allowbreak21,\allowbreak24,\allowbreak27\). The two-digit multiples have digit sums \(1+2=3\), \(1+5=6\), \(1+8=9\), \(2+1=3\), \(2+4=6\), and \(2+7=9\). Each sum is divisible by 3.

The larger examples also pass: 123 has digit sum \(1+2+3=6\), 372 has digit sum \(3+7+2=12\), and 1248 has digit sum \(1+2+4+8=15\). All three original numbers are divisible by 3.

The first nine positive multiples of 9 are \(9,\allowbreak18,\allowbreak27,\allowbreak36,\allowbreak45,\allowbreak54,\allowbreak63,\allowbreak72,\allowbreak81\). Each two-digit multiple has digit sum 9: \(1+8=9\), \(2+7=9\), \(3+6=9\), \(4+5=9\), \(5+4=9\), \(6+3=9\), \(7+2=9\), and \(8+1=9\).

The larger examples have digit sums \(1+1+7=9\), \(4+5+9=18\), \(1+2+0+6=9\), and \(8+0+1+9=18\). Thus 117, 459, 1206, and 8019 are all divisible by 9. A digit sum need not equal 9; it must be divisible by 9. Both tests use the digit sum, with a different divisor.

Tests using the digit sum

Add the digits. The original number is divisible by 3 exactly when the digit sum is divisible by 3; the same test works for 9. The digit sum is a test, not an equivalent value of the original number. Divisibility by 9 implies divisibility by 3, but not conversely.

Determine whether 1494 is divisible by 3 without doing long division.

Add the digits: \(1+4+9+4=18\). Since 18 is divisible by 3, 1494 is divisible by 3.

Determine whether 72816 is divisible by 9 without doing long division.

Add the digits: \(7+2+8+1+6=24\). Since 24 is not divisible by 9, 72816 is not divisible by 9.

Determine whether 583217 is divisible by 3 without doing long division.

Add the digits: \(5+8+3+2+1+7=26\). Since 26 is not divisible by 3, 583217 is not divisible by 3.

Determine whether 936 is divisible by 9 without doing long division.

Add the digits: \(9+3+6=18\). Since 18 is divisible by 9, 936 is divisible by 9.

Determine whether 219,552 is divisible by 3.

The sum of the digits is \(2+1+9+5+5+2=24\). Twenty-four is divisible by three, so 219,552 is divisible by three.

Determine whether 692,838 is divisible by 9.

The sum of the digits is \(6+9+2+8+3+8=36\). Thirty-six is divisible by nine, so 692,838 is divisible by nine.

Determine whether 820,297 is divisible by 9.

The sum of the digits is \(8+2+0+2+9+7=28\). Twenty-eight is not divisible by nine, so 820,297 is not divisible by nine.

Checking for a useful pattern

List the first seventeen positive multiples of 7. Compare their final digits and compute the digit sums of 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42. Explain why the final-digit and digit-sum tests used for 2, 3, 5, 9, and 10 cannot simply be reused for 7.

The first seventeen positive multiples of 7 are \(7,\allowbreak14,\allowbreak21,\allowbreak28,\allowbreak35,\allowbreak42,\allowbreak49,\allowbreak56,\allowbreak63,\allowbreak70,\allowbreak77,\allowbreak84,\allowbreak91,\allowbreak98,\allowbreak105,\allowbreak112,\allowbreak119\). Their final digits include every digit, so no small set of allowed final digits identifies divisibility by 7.

The requested digit sums are \(1+4=5\), \(2+1=3\), \(2+8=10\), \(3+5=8\), and \(4+2=6\). These sums are not divisible by 7 even though the original numbers are. We cannot reuse the tests for 2, 3, 5, 9, or 10; we need a different test or division itself.

When a quick test is unavailable

Long division is a prerequisite skill. If needed, review that procedure before these problems; the remainder decides whether the division is exact.

The properties for 2, 3, 5, 9, and 10 let us settle the question without performing division. When no convenient property has been provided for the divisor, use long division and inspect the remainder.

Determine whether 686 is divisible by 7.

Divide 686 by 7. The quotient is 98 with a remainder of 0. Because the remainder is 0, 686 is divisible by 7.

Determine whether 3529 is divisible by 7.

Divide 3529 by 7. The quotient is 504 with a remainder of 1. Because the remainder is not 0, 3529 is not divisible by 7.

Determine whether 524,652 is divisible by 7.

Use long division to divide the number by seven. The quotient is 74,950 with a remainder of two. Because the remainder is not zero, 524,652 is not divisible by seven.
