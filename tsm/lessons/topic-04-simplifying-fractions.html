Topic 4: Simplifying Fractions

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic creates equivalent fractions and reduces fractions to lowest terms by dividing the numerator and denominator by common factors.

Key definitions

Equivalent fractions: fractions with the same value. Numerator: the top number of a fraction. Denominator: the bottom number. Simplified fraction: a fraction whose numerator and denominator have no common factor greater than 1.

Write an equivalent fraction for one-half by multiplying the numerator and denominator by 2.

Begin with one half of a whole.

\(\frac{1}{2}\)

Imagine that the whole is divided into two equal pieces and one piece is shaded. Now divide each of those pieces into two smaller equal pieces. The whole is divided into four pieces, and two of them are shaded. The amount did not change; only the way the pieces were counted changed.

\(\frac{2}{4}\)

The two fractions therefore name the same number.

\(\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2}{4}\)

Numerically, this happens because the original fraction was multiplied by two over two. Two over two is one, and multiplying a number by one does not change its value.

\(\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{2}\)

Multiplying the numerators gives two, and multiplying the denominators gives four.

\(\frac{2}{4}\)

A fraction is a number, and a single number can have more than one fraction name. Changing the numerator and denominator does not necessarily change the amount represented. The important question is whether both parts of the fraction were changed in a way that preserves its value.

Equivalent fractions are different-looking fractions that name the same number. We can create an equivalent fraction by multiplying the numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number. We can also move in the opposite direction by dividing the numerator and denominator by the same common factor.

Write \(\frac{1}{10}\) as an equivalent fraction with denominator 30.

Suppose we want another name for one tenth, but the new denominator must be thirty.

\(\frac{1}{10} = \frac{?}{30}\)

Ask what multiplication changes ten into thirty. Ten must be multiplied by three. To preserve the value of the fraction, the numerator must also be multiplied by three. In other words, multiply by three over three, which is one.

\(\frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{3}{3}\)

The new numerator is three.

\(\frac{1}{10} = \frac{3}{30}\)

It would be incorrect to change only the denominator. Changing ten to thirty while leaving the numerator at one would create a different number, not another name for the same number.

Find an equivalent fraction for three eighths with denominator fifty-six.

\(\frac{3}{8} = \frac{?}{56}\)

Eight must be multiplied by seven to become fifty-six. Multiply the numerator by the same number.

\(\frac{3}{8} \cdot \frac{7}{7}\)

Three multiplied by seven is twenty-one.

\(\frac{3}{8} = \frac{21}{56}\)

Find an equivalent fraction for three sevenths with denominator eighty-four.

\(\frac{3}{7} = \frac{?}{84}\)

Seven must be multiplied by twelve to become eighty-four. The numerator must also be multiplied by twelve.

\(\frac{3}{7} \cdot \frac{12}{12}\)

The resulting numerator is thirty-six.

\(\frac{3}{7} = \frac{36}{84}\)

The requested denominator must be a whole-number multiple of the original denominator for this particular method to work. Once the needed multiplier is known, use that same multiplier in both the numerator and denominator.

Write the fraction in lowest terms.

\(\frac{30}{35}\)

Simplifying, reducing, and writing a fraction in lowest terms describe the same goal. We want an equivalent fraction whose numerator and denominator have no common factor greater than one. Rather than multiplying by a form of one, we divide out a factor that the numerator and denominator share.

Thirty and thirty-five are both divisible by five. Divide both by five. The numerator becomes six, and the denominator becomes seven.

\(\frac{6}{7}\)

Six and seven have no common factor greater than one, so the fraction is now in lowest terms.

Reducing in more than one stage

It is not necessary to notice the greatest common factor immediately. A fraction may be reduced a little at a time, as long as the numerator and denominator are divided by the same number at each stage.

\(\frac{18}{30}\)

Both numbers are even, so divide both by two.

\(\frac{9}{15}\)

The new numerator and denominator are both divisible by three. Divide both by three.

\(\frac{3}{5}\)

Three and five share no common factor greater than one. We could also have divided eighteen and thirty by six in one step, but the staged approach reaches the same equivalent fraction.

Write the fraction in lowest terms.

\(\frac{32}{200}\)

Both numbers are divisible by two.

\(\frac{16}{100}\)

They are still both divisible by two.

\(\frac{8}{50}\)

They are still both even, so divide by two once more.

\(\frac{4}{25}\)

Four and twenty-five have no common factor greater than one. The first successful reduction was not the end of the problem; the fraction had to be checked again after every change.

Write the fraction in lowest terms.

\(\frac{120}{150}\)

Both numbers end in zero, so dividing both by ten is an easy first reduction.

\(\frac{12}{15}\)

Twelve and fifteen are both divisible by three.

\(\frac{4}{5}\)

The final fraction is in lowest terms. Other valid common factors could have been used first, but every correct path must preserve the value and must continue until no common factor remains.

Equivalent-fraction work always preserves the number being represented. To create a requested larger denominator, multiply the numerator and denominator by the same number; this is multiplication by one. To reduce a fraction, divide the numerator and denominator by the same common factor, and keep checking until the only common factor is one.
