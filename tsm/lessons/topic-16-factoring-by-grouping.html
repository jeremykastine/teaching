TSM Lecture Notes

Topic 16: Factoring by Grouping

Assigned to: TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic factors four-term polynomials by pairing terms, factoring each pair, and then factoring the common binomial.

Key definitions

Factoring by grouping: a method that creates matching binomial factors from groups of terms. Common binomial factor: an identical two-term factor appearing in each grouped product.

Factor by grouping.

\[x^{3} + 9x^{2} + 3x + 27\]

Use the first two terms as one pair and the last two terms as the second pair. Do not rewrite those pairs inside parentheses. Instead, determine the GCF of each pair and write each GCF first. The new parentheses are opened only as part of that factoring step.

The GCF of the first group is \(x^{2}\). The GCF of the second group is \(3\).

\[x^{2}(x + 9) + 3(x + 9)\]

The important result is that both terms now contain exactly the same factor, \(x + 9\). Think of the expression above as two terms: one term is \(x^{2}\) times \(x + 9\), and the other is \(3\) times \(x + 9\). The entire quantity \(x + 9\) is their common factor.

\[(x + 9)\left( x^{2} + 3 \right)\]

Factoring by grouping therefore uses the GCF idea twice. First, factor a GCF from each small group. Then factor the matching parenthetical expression from the two resulting terms. Distributing the final product would recreate all four terms in the original expression.

Factoring by grouping continues the work from B7. A polynomial with four terms may not have one useful greatest common factor shared by all four terms. However, the terms can sometimes be treated as two pairs so that each pair has its own greatest common factor. We do not place parentheses around the unfactored pairs. Parentheses first appear only after a common factor has been written and factored out. After the GCF is removed from each pair, the same parenthetical expression should appear twice. That entire parenthetical expression is then a common factor and can be factored out.

Factor by grouping.

\[18x^{3} - 45x^{2} + 14x - 35\]

Use the first two terms as one pair and the last two terms as the second pair. Again, do not enclose the unfactored pairs in parentheses. The signs inside each new set of parentheses are determined only after we decide which common factor, including its sign, is being removed.

The first group has GCF \(9x^{2}\), and the second group has GCF \(7\). Keep the negative signs with the terms they belong to.

\[9x^{2}(2x - 5) + 7(2x - 5)\]

Both new terms contain the factor \(2x - 5\).

\[(2x - 5)\left( 9x^{2} + 7 \right)\]

The expressions inside the two sets of parentheses must match exactly before they can be factored out as one common factor.

Factor by grouping.

\[x^{3} + 4x^{2} + 7x + 28\]

Factor the GCF from each pair.

\[x^{2}(x + 4) + 7(x + 4)\]

The repeated factor is \(x + 4\).

\[(x + 4)\left( x^{2} + 7 \right)\]

Factor by grouping.

\[14x^{3} - 35x^{2} + 6x - 15\]

The first pair has GCF \(7x^{2}\), and the second pair has GCF \(3\).

\[7x^{2}(2x - 5) + 3(2x - 5)\]

Factor out the matching parenthetical expression.

\[(2x - 5)\left( 7x^{2} + 3 \right)\]

Factor by grouping.

\[8z^{2} + 24z - az - 3a\]

The first group factors naturally using \(8z\).

\[8z(z + 3) - az - 3a\]

For the last two terms, factor out negative \(a\). Factoring out positive \(a\) would leave \(- z - 3\), which would not match \(z + 3\). Factoring out negative \(a\) changes both signs inside that group.

\[8z(z + 3) - a(z + 3)\]

Now the two terms contain the same factor.

\[(z + 3)(8z - a)\]

When the two parenthetical expressions differ only because all their signs are opposite, check whether a negative GCF should be removed from one group.

Factor by grouping.

\[4 + 9xy - 18y - 2x\]

Grouping the terms exactly as written does not produce matching parenthetical factors. Since the expression is a sum of signed terms, the terms may be rearranged as long as every sign remains attached to its term. Place the two terms containing \(y\) first, and then place the remaining two terms together.

\[9xy - 18y - 2x + 4\]

The first pair has GCF \(9y\). From the second pair, factor out negative \(2\) so that the expression inside the parentheses will match.

\[9y(x - 2) - 2(x - 2)\]

The common factor is \(x - 2\).

\[(x - 2)(9y - 2)\]

Rearranging is not changing any term. It is only placing terms next to useful partners. A positive term remains positive, and a negative term remains negative.

Factor by grouping.

\[8st + 12t - 10s - 15\]

The first two terms share \(4t\). The last two terms share negative \(5\).

\[4t(2s + 3) - 5(2s + 3)\]

Factor out the repeated binomial.

\[(2s + 3)(4t - 5)\]

Factor by grouping.

\[4z^{2} + 16z - az - 4a\]

The first pair has GCF \(4z\). The second pair has GCF negative \(a\).

\[4z(z + 4) - a(z + 4)\]

Factor out the repeated binomial.

\[(z + 4)(4z - a)\]

Start by looking for four terms and identifying two useful pairs, but do not enclose the original pairs in parentheses. Find the greatest common factor of each pair. For each pair, write the common factor first and then open a new set of parentheses containing what remains. The goal is for the two resulting parenthetical expressions to be identical. When they match, factor that entire expression out just as you would factor out a common number or variable. When they do not match, recheck the GCFs and signs, consider factoring out a negative from one pair, and consider whether the signed terms need to be rearranged into more useful pairs. Finally, multiply the factors to verify that the original four-term polynomial is reproduced.
