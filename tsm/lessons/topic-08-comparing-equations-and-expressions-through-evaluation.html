Topic 8: Comparing Equations and Expressions Through Evaluation

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

Topic 7 taught us to calculate the value of an expression. Now we will compare two algebraic expressions, substituting the same number for the variable in each.

An expression such as \(2x+3\) can have different values depending on the number substituted for \(x\). When we compare two expressions, we ask: For which values of the variable do the two expressions give the same answer?

Everyone studies every problem. Problems marked Assessment: TSM 099 only go beyond what TSM 098 students will be assessed on. Students in both courses participate in the instruction and attempt every example.

Key definitions

Evaluate: substitute a number for the variable and calculate the result. Equivalent expressions: expressions that give the same answer whenever the calculations can be carried out. We can use one in place of the other, remembering that division by zero is never allowed.

Three possible relationships

For the algebraic expressions we study here, there are three main possibilities:

  1. Everywhere

    The expressions give the same answer for every value of the variable, so we can use one in place of the other.

    In certain cases, some x-values make a calculation impossible, so we must limit what we mean by “everywhere.” Even in those cases, the expressions agree for the entire set of x-values under consideration.

  2. At one or a few scattered values

    Certain numbers give matching answers, while other numbers give different answers.

  3. Nowhere

    No real number makes the two expressions give the same answer. Sometimes we can see why by thinking about positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero.

The first three problems illustrate these possibilities in order. Later comparisons use more involved expressions, but the question stays the same: do the two expressions give matching answers?

Matching answers at several chosen values can suggest that two expressions are equivalent, but those calculations alone do not prove it. One different answer is enough to show that they are not equivalent. Likewise, several different answers do not prove that a match is impossible.

This will help us later with equations: finding when two expressions match is the central question in solving an equation. For now, we will compare expressions by substitution and explain what we observe.

Compare the two expressions

For each chosen number, substitute it into both expressions and compare the answers. Keep the two calculations separate.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Describe the matching answers you observe. Explain why the distributive property tells us they will match for every number substituted.

\[2(x+3)\qquad\text{and}\qquad 2x+6\]

At \(x=-2\), the first expression gives \(2((-2)+3)=2(1)=2\) and the second gives \(2(-2)+6=-4+6=2\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(2(0+3)=2(3)=6\) and the second gives \(2(0)+6=0+6=6\). The values agree.

At \(x=3\), the first expression gives \(2(3+3)=2(6)=12\) and the second gives \(2(3)+6=6+6=12\). The values agree.

The distributive property gives \(2(x+3)=2x+6\). This works for every number substituted for \(x\), so these expressions match everywhere. We can use either one in place of the other. The three matching calculations illustrate this; the property explains why it is always true.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=1\), \(x=2\). Identify the chosen values that give matching answers and those that give different answers. Which of the three relationships does this illustrate?

\[x^2\qquad\text{and}\qquad 2x\]

At \(x=-1\), the first expression gives \((-1)^2=1\) and the second gives \(2(-1)=-2\). The values differ.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(0^2=0\) and the second gives \(2(0)=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), the first expression gives \(1^2=1\) and the second gives \(2(1)=2\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \(2^2=4\) and the second gives \(2(2)=4\). The values agree.

The expressions match when \(x=0\) and \(x=2\), but give different answers when \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\). They illustrate matches at a few scattered values. A match at one chosen value does not mean they always match.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Think about the signs of the answers. Could any real number make these expressions match?

\[x^2\qquad\text{and}\qquad -1\]

At \(x=-2\), the first expression gives \((-2)^2=4\) and the second gives \(-1\). The values differ.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(0^2=0\) and the second gives \(-1\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), the first expression gives \(3^2=9\) and the second gives \(-1\). The values differ.

Squaring a real number gives a positive number or zero. The other expression is always \(-1\), which is negative. The answers can never be the same: these expressions match nowhere. This reason covers every real number, rather than just the three we tried.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). Find a chosen value that gives matching answers and one that gives different answers. Does one match mean we can always use one expression in place of the other?

\[2(x+4)\qquad\text{and}\qquad 3x+5\]

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(2(0+4)=2(4)=8\) and the second gives \(3(0)+5=5\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \(2(2+4)=2(6)=12\) and the second gives \(3(2)+5=6+5=11\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), the first expression gives \(2(3+4)=2(7)=14\) and the second gives \(3(3)+5=9+5=14\). The values agree.

They match when \(x=3\), but not when \(x=0\) or \(x=2\). This illustrates a match at a scattered value. The different answers show that we cannot always replace one expression with the other.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\). Can we use either expression in place of the other? Give a reason that applies to every number substituted.

\[3(x-2)+x\qquad\text{and}\qquad 4x-6\]

At \(x=-1\), the first expression gives \(3((-1)-2)+(-1)=3(-3)-1=-10\) and the second gives \(4(-1)-6=-4-6=-10\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(3(0-2)+0=3(-2)=-6\) and the second gives \(4(0)-6=-6\). The values agree.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \(3(2-2)+2=3(0)+2=2\) and the second gives \(4(2)-6=8-6=2\). The values agree.

Distributing and collecting the multiples of \(x\) gives \(3(x-2)+x=3x-6+x=4x-6\). This works for every number substituted, so the expressions match everywhere and can be used in place of one another.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Calculate the answers by substitution, without expanding first. Then briefly explain why these expressions always match.

\[(x-3)(x+3)\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^2-9\]

At \(x=-2\), the first expression gives \(((-2)-3)((-2)+3)=(-5)(1)=-5\) and the second gives \((-2)^2-9=4-9=-5\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \((0-3)(0+3)=(-3)(3)=-9\) and the second gives \(0^2-9=-9\). The values agree.

At \(x=3\), the first expression gives \((3-3)(3+3)=(0)(6)=0\) and the second gives \(3^2-9=9-9=0\). The values agree.

Multiplication gives \((x-3)(x+3)=x^2+3x-3x-9=x^2-9\). The two middle terms cancel for every value of \(x\). These expressions match everywhere, so we can use either one in place of the other.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=0\), \(x=1\), \(x=4\). Use the matching and different answers to choose one of the three relationships. Explain how a match connects to our later work with equations.

\[x(x+2)\qquad\text{and}\qquad 3x\]

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(0(0+2)=0(2)=0\) and the second gives \(3(0)=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), the first expression gives \(1(1+2)=1(3)=3\) and the second gives \(3(1)=3\). The values agree.

At \(x=4\), the first expression gives \(4(4+2)=4(6)=24\) and the second gives \(3(4)=12\). The values differ.

The expressions match when \(x=0\) and \(x=1\), but not when \(x=4\). They illustrate matches at a few scattered values. Later, when we write an equation saying these expressions are equal, the matching values will be its solutions. Here we simply compare the answers at the numbers given.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=1\), \(x=2\). Compare the first three chosen values before trying the fourth. Would three matches be enough to show that the expressions always match?

\[(x+1)x(x-1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\]

At \(x=-1\), the first expression gives \(((-1)+1)(-1)((-1)-1)=(0)(-1)(-2)=0\) and the second gives \(0\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \((0+1)(0)(0-1)=(1)(0)(-1)=0\) and the second gives \(0\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), the first expression gives \((1+1)(1)(1-1)=(2)(1)(0)=0\) and the second gives \(0\). The values agree.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \((2+1)(2)(2-1)=(3)(2)(1)=6\) and the second gives \(0\). The values differ.

A zero factor makes the first three answers match. But when \(x=2\), the answers are 6 and 0. The expressions illustrate matches at a few scattered values. Three matches were not enough to show that they always match.

Assessment: TSM 099 only

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=1\). Compare the answers wherever the calculations are possible. What happens when \(x=1\)? Explain when we can use one expression in place of the other.

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad x+1\]

At \(x=-1\), the first expression gives \(\frac{(-1)^2-1}{(-1)-1}=\frac{0}{-2}=0\) and the second gives \((-1)+1=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and the second gives \(0+1=1\). The values agree.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \(\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and the second gives \(2+1=3\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), the first expression gives \(\frac{1^2-1}{1-1}=\frac{0}{0}\text{ (undefined)}\) and the second gives \(1+1=2\). There are not two numerical values to compare.

When \(x=1\), the first calculation would require division by zero, so it has no answer. The second expression gives 2. We must remember that the first expression cannot be used with \(x=1\).

For other values, the product \((x-1)(x+1)\) is \(x^2-1\). Dividing that product by the nonzero factor \(x-1\) leaves \(x+1\). Thus these expressions match everywhere that both calculations are possible. We may use \(x+1\) in place of the fraction, but we must still remember not to use \(x=1\) in the original calculation.

Assessment: TSM 099 only

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\). State which value would cause division by zero. Find matching and different answers, and choose one of the three relationships.

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{3}{x-1}\]

At \(x=-2\), the first expression gives \(\frac{(-2)^2-1}{(-2)-1}=\frac{3}{-3}=-1\) and the second gives \(\frac{3}{(-2)-1}=\frac{3}{-3}=-1\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and the second gives \(\frac{3}{0-1}=\frac{3}{-1}=-3\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \(\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and the second gives \(\frac{3}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\). The values agree.

The value \(x=1\) would cause division by zero in both expressions. All the chosen values avoid that problem. The expressions match at \(-2\) and \(2\) but not at \(0\), illustrating matches at a few scattered values.

Assessment: TSM 099 only

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-3\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). State which value would cause division by zero. Compare the first three chosen values before trying the fourth. Do three different answers mean that a match is impossible?

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{2x+2}{x-1}\]

At \(x=-3\), the first expression gives \(\frac{(-3)^2-1}{(-3)-1}=\frac{8}{-4}=-2\) and the second gives \(\frac{2(-3)+2}{(-3)-1}=\frac{-4}{-4}=1\). The values differ.

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \(\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and the second gives \(\frac{2(0)+2}{0-1}=\frac{2}{-1}=-2\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \(\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and the second gives \(\frac{2(2)+2}{2-1}=\frac{6}{1}=6\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), the first expression gives \(\frac{3^2-1}{3-1}=\frac{8}{2}=4\) and the second gives \(\frac{2(3)+2}{3-1}=\frac{8}{2}=4\). The values agree.

The value \(x=1\) would cause division by zero. The first three chosen values give different answers, but \(x=3\) gives a match. The expressions illustrate matches at scattered values. Several different answers do not show that the expressions match nowhere.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). Explain why the expressions cannot match for any real number. Use what you know about squaring, rather than just the answers you calculated.

\[(x-2)^2+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\]

At \(x=0\), the first expression gives \((0-2)^2+1=(-2)^2+1=4+1=5\) and the second gives \(0\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), the first expression gives \((2-2)^2+1=0^2+1=1\) and the second gives \(0\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), the first expression gives \((3-2)^2+1=1^2+1=2\) and the second gives \(0\). The values differ.

The square \((x-2)^2\) is always positive or zero. Adding 1 makes the first answer at least 1, while the second answer is 0. These expressions match nowhere. This reason explains why no real number can produce a match.
