Topic 8: Comparing Equations and Expressions Through Evaluation

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

Topic 7 taught us to calculate the value of an expression. Now we will compare two algebraic expressions, substituting the same number for the variable in each.

An expression such as \(2x+3\) can have different values depending on the number substituted for \(x\). Putting an equals sign between two expressions gives an equation. We ask: For which values of the variable, if any, is this equation true?

Everyone studies every problem. Problems marked Assessment: TSM 099 only go beyond what TSM 098 students will be assessed on. Students in both courses participate in the instruction and attempt every example.

Key definitions

Evaluate: substitute a number for the variable and calculate the result. Equation: a statement that two expressions are equal. A solution is a value of the variable that makes the equation true: both sides can be calculated and give the same answer.

Identity: an equation that is true for every value of the variable under consideration. Its two sides are equivalent expressions: we can use one in place of the other, remembering that division by zero is never allowed.

Three possible outcomes of an equation

We will compare two algebraic expressions with a single variable, \(x\), by putting an equals sign between them. The equals sign states that the expressions have the same value; we still need to decide when that statement is true. For the equations studied here, there are three possibilities:

  1. The equation is true for every value of \(x\).

    The expressions give the same answer every time. This is an identity, and the expressions are interchangeable. Sometimes we must limit what “every” means: it means every value of \(x\) under consideration in the problem. Certain values may need to be excluded because they make a calculation impossible.

  2. The equation is true for one or a few scattered values of \(x\).

    The expressions give the same answer at those values, but different answers at other values. The matching values are solutions, but the expressions are not interchangeable for every value of \(x\).

  3. The equation is true for no value of \(x\).

    No value of \(x\) under consideration makes the two expressions give the same answer. The equation has no solution.

The first three problems illustrate these possibilities in order: always true, sometimes true, never true. Later equations use more involved expressions, but the question stays the same.

Matching answers at several chosen values can suggest an identity, but those calculations alone do not prove it. One different answer is enough to show that an equation is not an identity. Likewise, several different answers do not prove that an equation has no solution.

When is the equation true?

For each problem, evaluate the left and right sides separately at every chosen value. State which substitutions make the equation true and which make it false. Decide whether the equation is always true (an identity with interchangeable expressions), sometimes true, or never true. Use the requested explanation to support your conclusion; you do not need a general equation-solving method yet.

Evaluate both sides at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Is this equation an identity? Explain why the two expressions can or cannot be used in place of one another for every value of \(x\).

\[3(2x-5)+4=6x-11\]

At \(x=-2\), the left side gives \(3(2(-2)-5)+4=3(-9)+4=-27+4=-23\) and the right side gives \(6(-2)-11=-12-11=-23\). The equation is true.

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \(3(2(0)-5)+4=3(-5)+4=-15+4=-11\) and the right side gives \(6(0)-11=-11\). The equation is true.

At \(x=3\), the left side gives \(3(2(3)-5)+4=3(1)+4=7\) and the right side gives \(6(3)-11=18-11=7\). The equation is true.

Distributing gives \(3(2x-5)+4=6x-15+4=6x-11\). These steps apply to every value of \(x\), so the equation is always true: an identity. The three substitutions illustrate it; the distributive property and combining constants explain why it is always true. We may replace the longer expression \(3(2x-5)+4\) with the simpler expression \(6x-11\), or use the longer expression in place of the shorter one.

Evaluate both sides at \(x=-1\), \(x=1\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). Which chosen values are solutions? Is the equation always, sometimes, or never true? Can the two expressions be used interchangeably?

\[2x^2+6=6x+2\]

At \(x=-1\), the left side gives \(2(-1)^2+6=2(1)+6=8\) and the right side gives \(6(-1)+2=-6+2=-4\). The equation is false.

At \(x=1\), the left side gives \(2(1)^2+6=2+6=8\) and the right side gives \(6(1)+2=8\). The equation is true.

At \(x=2\), the left side gives \(2(2)^2+6=2(4)+6=14\) and the right side gives \(6(2)+2=14\). The equation is true.

At \(x=3\), the left side gives \(2(3)^2+6=2(9)+6=24\) and the right side gives \(6(3)+2=20\). The equation is false.

The equation is sometimes true. The chosen values \(x=1\) and \(x=2\) are solutions; \(x=-1\) and \(x=3\) are not. Although both sides use 2, 6, and \(x\), their similar appearance does not make them interchangeable. The different answers show that this equation is not an identity.

Evaluate both sides at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Is the equation always, sometimes, or never true? Explain whether any value of \(x\) can make it true, and identify the distribution error suggested by the right side.

\[2(3x+1)=6x+3\]

At \(x=-2\), the left side gives \(2(3(-2)+1)=2(-5)=-10\) and the right side gives \(6(-2)+3=-12+3=-9\). The equation is false.

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \(2(3(0)+1)=2(1)=2\) and the right side gives \(6(0)+3=3\). The equation is false.

At \(x=3\), the left side gives \(2(3(3)+1)=2(10)=20\) and the right side gives \(6(3)+3=18+3=21\). The equation is false.

Correct distribution gives \(2(3x+1)=6x+2\). The right side of the proposed equation is \(6x+3\), which is always 1 greater. Equality would require \(2=3\), so the equation is never true: it has no solution. This reasoning covers every value, rather than just the three tested.

The right side suggests multiplying the first term correctly, \(2(3x)=6x\), but adding to the second term, \(2+1=3\). Distribution requires multiplying both terms: the second term should be \(2(1)=2\). The expressions are not interchangeable.

Evaluate both sides of the equation at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Calculate the answers by substitution, without expanding first. Then classify the equation as always, sometimes, or never true and explain whether the expressions are interchangeable.

\[(x-3)(x+3)=x^2-9\]

At \(x=-2\), the left side gives \(((-2)-3)((-2)+3)=(-5)(1)=-5\) and the right side gives \((-2)^2-9=4-9=-5\). The equation is true.

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \((0-3)(0+3)=(-3)(3)=-9\) and the right side gives \(0^2-9=-9\). The equation is true.

At \(x=3\), the left side gives \((3-3)(3+3)=(0)(6)=0\) and the right side gives \(3^2-9=9-9=0\). The equation is true.

Multiplication gives \((x-3)(x+3)=x^2+3x-3x-9=x^2-9\). The two middle terms cancel for every value of \(x\). The equation is always true: an identity, so we can use either expression in place of the other.

Evaluate both sides of the equation at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=1\), \(x=2\). Compare the first three chosen values before trying the fourth. Would three true substitutions be enough to establish an identity? Classify the equation as always, sometimes, or never true, and explain whether the expressions are interchangeable.

\[(x+1)x(x-1)=0\]

At \(x=-1\), the left side gives \(((-1)+1)(-1)((-1)-1)=(0)(-1)(-2)=0\) and the right side gives \(0\). The equation is true.

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \((0+1)(0)(0-1)=(1)(0)(-1)=0\) and the right side gives \(0\). The equation is true.

At \(x=1\), the left side gives \((1+1)(1)(1-1)=(2)(1)(0)=0\) and the right side gives \(0\). The equation is true.

At \(x=2\), the left side gives \((2+1)(2)(2-1)=(3)(2)(1)=6\) and the right side gives \(0\). The equation is false.

A zero factor makes the first three answers match. But when \(x=2\), the answers are 6 and 0. The equation is sometimes true: \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), and \(x=1\) are solutions, but \(x=2\) is not. Three true substitutions were not enough to establish an identity. The expressions are not interchangeable.

Assessment: TSM 099 only

Evaluate both sides of the equation at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=1\). State whether each substitution makes the equation true, false, or undefined. What happens when \(x=1\)? Decide whether this is an identity for the values under consideration and explain when the expressions are interchangeable.

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=x+1\]

At \(x=-1\), the left side gives \(\frac{(-1)^2-1}{(-1)-1}=\frac{0}{-2}=0\) and the right side gives \((-1)+1=0\). The equation is true.

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \(\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and the right side gives \(0+1=1\). The equation is true.

At \(x=2\), the left side gives \(\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and the right side gives \(2+1=3\). The equation is true.

At \(x=1\), the left side gives \(\frac{1^2-1}{1-1}=\frac{0}{0}\text{ (undefined)}\) and the right side gives \(1+1=2\). The equation is undefined at this value; \(x=1\) is excluded from consideration and is not a solution.

When \(x=1\), the first calculation would require division by zero, so it has no answer. The second expression gives 2. We must remember that the first expression cannot be used with \(x=1\).

For other values, the product \((x-1)(x+1)\) is \(x^2-1\). Dividing that product by the nonzero factor \(x-1\) leaves \(x+1\). Thus the equation is always true: an identity for every value under consideration, with \(x=1\) excluded. We may use \(x+1\) in place of the fraction, but we must still remember not to use \(x=1\) in the original calculation.

Assessment: TSM 099 only

Evaluate both sides of the equation at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\). State which value would cause division by zero. Identify which chosen values are solutions. Classify the equation as always, sometimes, or never true, and explain whether the expressions are interchangeable.

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=\frac{3}{x-1}\]

At \(x=-2\), the left side gives \(\frac{(-2)^2-1}{(-2)-1}=\frac{3}{-3}=-1\) and the right side gives \(\frac{3}{(-2)-1}=\frac{3}{-3}=-1\). The equation is true.

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \(\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and the right side gives \(\frac{3}{0-1}=\frac{3}{-1}=-3\). The equation is false.

At \(x=2\), the left side gives \(\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and the right side gives \(\frac{3}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\). The equation is true.

The value \(x=1\) would cause division by zero in both expressions. All the chosen values avoid that problem. The equation is sometimes true: \(x=-2\) and \(x=2\) are solutions, but \(x=0\) is not. The expressions are not interchangeable.

Evaluate both sides of the equation at \(x=-3\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). State which value would cause division by zero. Compare the first three chosen values before trying the fourth. Do three false substitutions mean the equation has no solution? Classify it as always, sometimes, or never true, and explain whether the expressions are interchangeable.

\[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=\frac{2x+2}{x-1}\]

At \(x=-3\), the left side gives \(\frac{(-3)^2-1}{(-3)-1}=\frac{8}{-4}=-2\) and the right side gives \(\frac{2(-3)+2}{(-3)-1}=\frac{-4}{-4}=1\). The equation is false.

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \(\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and the right side gives \(\frac{2(0)+2}{0-1}=\frac{2}{-1}=-2\). The equation is false.

At \(x=2\), the left side gives \(\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and the right side gives \(\frac{2(2)+2}{2-1}=\frac{6}{1}=6\). The equation is false.

At \(x=3\), the left side gives \(\frac{3^2-1}{3-1}=\frac{8}{2}=4\) and the right side gives \(\frac{2(3)+2}{3-1}=\frac{8}{2}=4\). The equation is true.

The value \(x=1\) would cause division by zero. The first three chosen values give different answers, but \(x=3\) gives a match. The equation is sometimes true: \(x=3\) is a solution. It is not an identity, so the expressions are not interchangeable. Three false substitutions did not show that the equation had no solution.

Evaluate both sides at \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). Is the equation always, sometimes, or never true? Explain whether any value of \(x\) can make it true by comparing the expressions after expanding the square.

\[(x-2)^2+1=x^2-4x+6\]

At \(x=0\), the left side gives \((0-2)^2+1=(-2)^2+1=5\) and the right side gives \(0^2-4(0)+6=6\). The equation is false.

At \(x=2\), the left side gives \((2-2)^2+1=0^2+1=1\) and the right side gives \(2^2-4(2)+6=4-8+6=2\). The equation is false.

At \(x=3\), the left side gives \((3-2)^2+1=1^2+1=2\) and the right side gives \(3^2-4(3)+6=9-12+6=3\). The equation is false.

The square means \((x-2)(x-2)\). Multiplying gives \(x^2-2x-2x+4=x^2-4x+4\), so the full left side is \((x-2)^2+1=x^2-4x+5\). The right side is \(x^2-4x+6\), always 1 greater. Equality would require \(5=6\). The equation is never true: it has no solution, and the expressions are not interchangeable. The expanded forms explain why no value works; three false substitutions alone would not prove that.
