Topic 8: Comparing Equations and Expressions Through Evaluation

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

Topic 7 asks: How do we calculate the value of an expression at a chosen input? This topic asks: What can we discover by comparing the values of two expressions at the same input?

An expression containing a variable generally does not have one fixed numerical value. For example, the value of \(2x+3\) changes as the input changes. Some expressions, such as a constant, keep the same value. We will evaluate pairs of expressions using the same input and ask: For which inputs, if any, do the two expressions produce the same value?

Everyone studies every problem. Problems marked Assessment: TSM 099 only exceed the assessment expectations for TSM 098. Students in both courses participate in the instruction and attempt every example; the label describes what TSM 099 students may be asked to demonstrate independently on tests.

Key definitions

Input: a value substituted for a variable. Domain: the inputs for which an expression is defined. Common domain: the inputs for which both expressions are defined. Equivalent expressions on a stated domain: expressions producing the same value for every input in that domain. Restriction: an input excluded from a domain, such as one that makes a denominator zero.

Three possible relationships

  1. Always or almost always equal. The expressions agree throughout their common domain, possibly with excluded inputs that must be noted. Such expressions are interchangeable for calculations on that common domain. Agreement there does not make their natural domains identical or permit unrestricted replacement.
  2. Sometimes equal. The expressions agree at some allowable inputs and differ at others. Elementary algebra often gives just one, two, or three isolated matching inputs, but this is not a general rule: more complicated expressions can agree at infinitely many inputs without agreeing everywhere.
  3. Never equal. No input in the common domain gives matching values. Reasoning about possible signs or ranges of values can sometimes establish this.

Our comparisons use real inputs and real values. Check restrictions first; an undefined expression does not supply a value to compare. The first three problems illustrate the three relationships in order, and the remaining comparisons develop the reasoning and calculations.

In later topics, an equation will state that two expressions are equal, and solving it will mean identifying all allowable matching inputs. Equivalent expressions will also help us rewrite larger expressions and calculations. Here the task is to evaluate, compare, and justify conclusions; those later procedures are not needed.

Compare, observe, and explain

In each problem, \(A(x)\) and \(B(x)\) are short names for the two expressions' values at input \(x\). Substitute the same input into both. Numerical matches and disagreements are observations; a conclusion about every input needs a reason that applies to every relevant input.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Describe the agreement you observe. Explain why the distributive property supports a conclusion about every real input.

\[A(x)=2(x+3)\qquad B(x)=2x+6\]

At \(x=-2\), \(A(-2)=2((-2)+3)=2(1)=2\) and \(B(-2)=2(-2)+6=-4+6=2\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=2(0+3)=2(3)=6\) and \(B(0)=2(0)+6=0+6=6\). The values agree.

At \(x=3\), \(A(3)=2(3+3)=2(6)=12\) and \(B(3)=2(3)+6=6+6=12\). The values agree.

Three matches suggest agreement, but the calculations alone do not prove it for all inputs. The distributive property gives \(2(x+3)=2x+6\) for every real input. Both expressions are defined for every real number, so they are interchangeable there. This pair belongs to always or almost always equal, with no excluded inputs.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=1\), \(x=2\). Identify matching and nonmatching inputs among those supplied. Which of the three relationships is established by these observations?

\[A(x)=x^2\qquad B(x)=2x\]

At \(x=-1\), \(A(-1)=(-1)^2=1\) and \(B(-1)=2(-1)=-2\). The values differ.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=0^2=0\) and \(B(0)=2(0)=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), \(A(1)=1^2=1\) and \(B(1)=2(1)=2\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=2^2=4\) and \(B(2)=2(2)=4\). The values agree.

The supplied matching inputs are \(0\) and \(2\); the nonmatching inputs are \(-1\) and \(1\). At least one match and at least one disagreement establish sometimes equal. The tests do not, by themselves, show that the matching inputs listed are the only ones. No equation-solving procedure is needed.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Use the possible signs of the two values to explain whether any real input could make them agree.

\[A(x)=x^2\qquad B(x)=-1\]

At \(x=-2\), \(A(-2)=(-2)^2=4\) and \(B(-2)=-1\). The values differ.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=0^2=0\) and \(B(0)=-1\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), \(A(3)=3^2=9\) and \(B(3)=-1\). The values differ.

Every real square is nonnegative, while \(-1\) is negative. Therefore the expressions are never equal for real inputs. The sign argument covers all real inputs; three disagreements alone would not establish this.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). Find a matching input and a nonmatching input among those supplied. Explain why one matching input does not make the expressions interchangeable.

\[A(x)=2(x+4)\qquad B(x)=3x+5\]

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=2(0+4)=2(4)=8\) and \(B(0)=3(0)+5=5\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=2(2+4)=2(6)=12\) and \(B(2)=3(2)+5=6+5=11\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), \(A(3)=2(3+4)=2(7)=14\) and \(B(3)=3(3)+5=9+5=14\). The values agree.

They agree at \(x=3\) and disagree at \(x=0\) and \(x=2\), so they are sometimes equal. Either disagreement disproves equivalence over the real numbers. A single matching input establishes agreement at that input, but cannot distinguish always equal from sometimes equal without further information.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\). Decide whether the expressions are interchangeable for real inputs. Give a reason that reaches beyond the tested values.

\[A(x)=3(x-2)+x\qquad B(x)=4x-6\]

At \(x=-1\), \(A(-1)=3((-1)-2)+(-1)=3(-3)-1=-10\) and \(B(-1)=4(-1)-6=-4-6=-10\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=3(0-2)+0=3(-2)=-6\) and \(B(0)=4(0)-6=-6\). The values agree.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=3(2-2)+2=3(0)+2=2\) and \(B(2)=4(2)-6=8-6=2\). The values agree.

Distributing the 3 and combining the two multiples of \(x\) gives \(3(x-2)+x=3x-6+x=4x-6\). These properties apply to every real input. Thus the expressions are always equal and interchangeable for real inputs; the three numerical matches illustrate that fact rather than prove it.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=3\). Compare the values without expanding before substitution. Then explain briefly why the product structure guarantees agreement for every real input.

\[A(x)=(x-3)(x+3)\qquad B(x)=x^2-9\]

At \(x=-2\), \(A(-2)=((-2)-3)((-2)+3)=(-5)(1)=-5\) and \(B(-2)=(-2)^2-9=4-9=-5\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=(0-3)(0+3)=(-3)(3)=-9\) and \(B(0)=0^2-9=-9\). The values agree.

At \(x=3\), \(A(3)=(3-3)(3+3)=(0)(6)=0\) and \(B(3)=3^2-9=9-9=0\). The values agree.

The product pairs the same input with opposite constants. Its two middle products cancel: \((x-3)(x+3)=x^2+3x-3x-9=x^2-9\). This reasoning applies to every real input, so the pair is always equal. Direct substitution was enough to obtain the numerical values; the brief product explanation justifies interchangeability.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=0\), \(x=1\), \(x=4\). Classify the relationship using both matching and nonmatching values. Explain what a match would mean in a later lesson about equations.

\[A(x)=x(x+2)\qquad B(x)=3x\]

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=0(0+2)=0(2)=0\) and \(B(0)=3(0)=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), \(A(1)=1(1+2)=1(3)=3\) and \(B(1)=3(1)=3\). The values agree.

At \(x=4\), \(A(4)=4(4+2)=4(6)=24\) and \(B(4)=3(4)=12\). The values differ.

The pair is sometimes equal: \(0\) and \(1\) give matches, while \(4\) gives a disagreement. In a later equation lesson, an input producing a match would be called a solution of the equation formed by setting these expressions equal. Here we are evaluating supplied inputs, without finding all such inputs.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=1\), \(x=2\). First compare the values at the first three inputs. Would those three matches prove interchangeability? Use the fourth input to test that conclusion.

\[A(x)=(x+1)x(x-1)\qquad B(x)=0\]

At \(x=-1\), \(A(-1)=((-1)+1)(-1)((-1)-1)=(0)(-1)(-2)=0\) and \(B(-1)=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=(0+1)(0)(0-1)=(1)(0)(-1)=0\) and \(B(0)=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), \(A(1)=(1+1)(1)(1-1)=(2)(1)(0)=0\) and \(B(1)=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=(2+1)(2)(2-1)=(3)(2)(1)=6\) and \(B(2)=0\). The values differ.

A zero factor explains each of the first three matches. Nevertheless, the disagreement at \(x=2\) disproves equivalence. The pair is sometimes equal. Even three distinct matching inputs do not prove that arbitrary expressions are interchangeable.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-1\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=1\). Compare the values wherever both exist, and identify any excluded input. Are they interchangeable on their common domain? Would replacing the first expression with the second allow the original restriction to be forgotten?

\[A(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\qquad B(x)=x+1\]

At \(x=-1\), \(A(-1)=\frac{(-1)^2-1}{(-1)-1}=\frac{0}{-2}=0\) and \(B(-1)=(-1)+1=0\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and \(B(0)=0+1=1\). The values agree.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and \(B(2)=2+1=3\). The values agree.

At \(x=1\), \(A(1)=\frac{1^2-1}{1-1}=\frac{0}{0}\text{ (undefined)}\) and \(B(1)=1+1=2\). There are not two numerical values to compare.

The original denominator excludes \(x=1\). On the common domain \(x\ne1\), the product relation \(x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)\) explains why dividing by the nonzero factor \(x-1\) gives \(x+1\). This brief property argument establishes agreement throughout that common domain; the tested matches alone do not.

This is always or almost always equal, with a domain exception. The natural domains differ, so these are not unrestricted replacements for one another. For example, replacing the first expression inside a larger calculation is valid for \(x\ne1\), but the original exclusion must remain. At \(x=1\), the second expression has a value and the first does not; undefined is not a numerical value and is not a disagreement between two values.

Assessment: TSM 099 only

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-2\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\). State the excluded input. Find matches and a disagreement, and classify the pair without solving an equation.

\[A(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\qquad B(x)=\frac{3}{x-1}\]

At \(x=-2\), \(A(-2)=\frac{(-2)^2-1}{(-2)-1}=\frac{3}{-3}=-1\) and \(B(-2)=\frac{3}{(-2)-1}=\frac{3}{-3}=-1\). The values agree.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and \(B(0)=\frac{3}{0-1}=\frac{3}{-1}=-3\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and \(B(2)=\frac{3}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\). The values agree.

Both original denominators exclude \(x=1\); all supplied inputs are allowed. The pair is sometimes equal on its common domain: it matches at \(-2\) and \(2\) and differs at \(0\). No factoring, cancellation, or equation-solving method was required to make these comparisons.

Assessment: TSM 099 only

Compare the two expressions at \(x=-3\), \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). State the excluded input. Compare the first three inputs before testing the fourth. Explain whether three disagreements would justify a conclusion of never equal.

\[A(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}\qquad B(x)=\frac{2x+2}{x-1}\]

At \(x=-3\), \(A(-3)=\frac{(-3)^2-1}{(-3)-1}=\frac{8}{-4}=-2\) and \(B(-3)=\frac{2(-3)+2}{(-3)-1}=\frac{-4}{-4}=1\). The values differ.

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=\frac{0^2-1}{0-1}=\frac{-1}{-1}=1\) and \(B(0)=\frac{2(0)+2}{0-1}=\frac{2}{-1}=-2\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=\frac{2^2-1}{2-1}=\frac{3}{1}=3\) and \(B(2)=\frac{2(2)+2}{2-1}=\frac{6}{1}=6\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), \(A(3)=\frac{3^2-1}{3-1}=\frac{8}{2}=4\) and \(B(3)=\frac{2(3)+2}{3-1}=\frac{8}{2}=4\). The values agree.

Both denominators exclude \(x=1\). The first three disagreements rule out equivalence but do not establish impossibility of agreement. The fourth input supplies a match, so the pair is sometimes equal. A finite collection of failed tests can miss an input at which the expressions agree.

Compare the two expressions at \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=3\). Explain why no allowable real input can produce a match. Base the conclusion on the possible values, rather than on the number of tests.

\[A(x)=(x-2)^2+1\qquad B(x)=0\]

At \(x=0\), \(A(0)=(0-2)^2+1=(-2)^2+1=4+1=5\) and \(B(0)=0\). The values differ.

At \(x=2\), \(A(2)=(2-2)^2+1=0^2+1=1\) and \(B(2)=0\). The values differ.

At \(x=3\), \(A(3)=(3-2)^2+1=1^2+1=2\) and \(B(3)=0\). The values differ.

For every real input, \((x-2)^2\) is nonnegative. Adding 1 makes the first expression at least 1, while the second is 0. Their possible values do not overlap. Therefore the pair is never equal. Unlike a list of numerical disagreements, this argument addresses every real input.
