Topic 8: Comparing Equations and Expressions Through Evaluation

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic evaluates both sides of equations at selected inputs to test solutions and gather evidence about whether two expressions agree.

Key definitions

Expression: numbers, variables, and operations without an equals sign. Equation: a statement that two expressions are equal. Solution: a value that makes an equation true. Identity: an equation true for every allowable input. Restriction: a value excluded from an expression's domain.

Evaluating an expression

An expression represents a value, while an equation states that two expressions are equal. To evaluate an expression, replace every occurrence of the variable with the given number and follow the order of operations. Parentheses around a substituted negative value are essential.

Evaluate \(x^2-3x+2\) for \(x=-2\).

\[( - 2)^{2} - 3( - 2) + 2 = 4 + 6 + 2 = 12.\]

Writing \(- 2^{2}\) instead would mean \(- \left( 2^{2} \right) = - 4\), so the parentheses protect the intended substitution.

Checking equations and identities

A value solves an equation when substitution makes both sides equal. One successful test does not prove an identity, which must hold for every allowable input. Algebraic properties can establish an identity. Never use an input that makes a denominator zero.

Determine whether \(x=3\) solves \(2(x+4)=3x+5\).

Evaluate the left side (\(2(3+4)=14\)) and the right side (\(3(3)+5=14\)). The sides agree, so this is a solution.

Determine whether \(x=2\) solves \(2(x+4)=3x+5\).

Evaluate the left side (\(2(2+4)=12\)) and the right side (\(3(2)+5=11\)). The sides differ, so this is not a solution.

Determine whether \(x^2=2x\) is an identity. Explain.

One counterexample is enough to disprove an identity. At \(x=3\), the left side is 9 and the right side is 6. They differ, so the equation is not an identity.

Determine whether \(3(x+2)=3x+6\) is an identity. Explain.

Distribute 3 to both terms: \(3(x+2)=3x+6\). The property holds for every real input, so this is an identity.

Determine whether \(x=4\) is an allowable input for \(\frac{x+1}{x-4}\).

The denominator becomes \(4-4=0\). Division by zero is undefined, so the input is not allowable.

Evaluate \(2x^2-5x+1\) at \(x=3\).

Substitute with parentheses, evaluate the power, then combine.

\[2(3)^2-5(3)+1=18-15+1=4\]

Evaluate \(2x^2-5x+1\) at \(x=-3\).

Substitute with parentheses, evaluate the power, then combine.

\[2(-3)^2-5(-3)+1=18+15+1=34\]

Determine whether \(x=1\) solves \(x(x+2)=3x\).

Evaluate the left side (\(1(1+2)=3\)) and the right side (\(3(1)=3\)). The sides are equal, so the value is a solution. This alone does not prove an identity.

Determine whether \(x=4\) solves \(x(x+2)=3x\).

Evaluate the left side (\(4(4+2)=24\)) and the right side (\(3(4)=12\)). The sides differ, so the value is not a solution. This also disproves an identity.
