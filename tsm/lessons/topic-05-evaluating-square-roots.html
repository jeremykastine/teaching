Topic 5: Evaluating Powers and Square Roots

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic evaluates powers with positive, zero, and negative integer exponents, including negative and fractional bases. It then connects squaring with square roots, evaluates roots of perfect squares, and distinguishes real and complex answers.

Key definitions

Base: the quantity being raised to a power. Exponent: for a positive integer exponent, the number of copies of the base used as factors. Power: an expression consisting of a base raised to an exponent. A zero exponent on a nonzero base gives 1; a negative integer exponent gives a reciprocal. Square root: a number that produces the radicand when multiplied by itself. Radicand: the expression beneath a radical sign. Perfect square: the square of an integer. Imaginary unit: i, defined by i squared equals negative 1.

Reading powers

For a positive integer exponent, the base is the repeated factor and the exponent counts its copies. An expansion writes those factors out; the value is their product. An exponent is not a multiplier of the base. As an exponent decreases by one, divide the preceding value by the nonzero base. Continuing this pattern gives \(a^0=1\) for a nonzero base.

Identify the base and exponent, expand, and evaluate \(3^4\).

The base is 3 and the exponent is 4, so use four factors of 3.

\[3^4=3\cdot3\cdot3\cdot3=81\]

Use the exponent pattern to determine the value of 3 raised to the zero power.

Begin with \(3^3=27\). Each decrease of one in the exponent divides the value by 3.

The next power is \(3^2=9\) (\(27\div3=9\)), followed by \(3^1=3\) (\(9\div3=3\)).

Continue that pattern from exponent 1 to exponent 0 (\(3\div3=1\)):

\[3^0=1\]

An exponent of zero does not mean multiplying the base by zero. The value must fit the same divide-by-3 pattern.

Use the exponent pattern to evaluate \(3^{-3}\).

Starting at \(3^0=1\), divide by 3 each time the exponent decreases by one. This gives \(3^{-1}=\frac13\) and then \(3^{-2}=\frac19\). Dividing by 3 once more gives the requested value:

\[3^{-3}=\frac1{27}\]

Evaluate \(5^{-2}\).

A negative exponent gives a reciprocal.

\[5^{-2}=\frac1{5^2}=\frac1{25}\]

Signs, parentheses, and negative exponents

Parentheses determine whether a negative sign is part of the base. A negative base has a positive value for an even integer exponent and a negative value for an odd integer exponent. A nonzero base to the zero power equals 1. A negative exponent indicates a reciprocal, not a negative value: \(a^{-n}=1/a^n\) for \(a\ne0\).

Identify the base and evaluate the power.

\[(-4)^2\]

Parentheses make the negative sign part of the base. The base is \(-4\).

The exponent applies to the entire base inside the parentheses. Therefore, the expansion is

\[( - 4)( - 4).\]

The value is

\[16.\]

Evaluate \((-4)^3\).

Three negative factors leave one unpaired negative sign.

\[(-4)^3=(-4)(-4)(-4)=-64\]

Evaluate \((-4)^{-3}\).

Take the reciprocal of the positive power; its sign is still negative.

\[(-4)^{-3}=\frac1{(-4)^3}=-\frac1{64}\]

Compare and contrast these two expressions: evaluate each and explain how the parentheses affect the result.

\[(-4)^2\]

\[-4^2\]

In the first expression, parentheses make \(-4\) the complete base. Squaring repeats both the number and its sign:

\[(-4)^2=(-4)(-4)=16\]

In the second expression, the base is only \(4\). Evaluate that power first; the negative sign remains outside it:

\[-4^2=-(4^2)=-(4\cdot4)=-16\]

The parentheses determine whether the negative sign is squared.

Compare and contrast \((-4)^0\) and \(-4^0\). Evaluate each and explain the role of parentheses.

The first expression has base negative four. In the second, the minus sign is outside the power.

\[(-4)^0=1\]

\[-4^0=-(4^0)=-1\]

Compare and contrast these two expressions: evaluate each and explain how the parentheses affect the result.

\[(-4)^{-2}\]

\[-4^{-2}\]

\[(-4)^{-2}=\frac{1}{(-4)^2}=\frac{1}{16}.\]

For the expression without parentheses,

\[- 4^{- 2} = - \frac{1}{16}.\]

When the negative sign is inside parentheses, it is part of the base and is repeated. When the negative sign is outside the power, it is not repeated. The exponent is evaluated first, and the outside negative sign is applied afterward.

Fractional bases

A fraction in parentheses is one complete base. For a positive exponent, repeat the entire fraction as a factor. For a negative exponent, take its reciprocal and use the corresponding positive exponent. The original base must be nonzero.

Evaluate \(\left(\frac54\right)^{2}\).

Multiply the numerator factors together and the denominator factors together.

\[\left(\frac54\right)^{2}=\frac{5^2}{4^2}=\frac{25}{16}\]

Evaluate \(\left(\frac54\right)^{3}\).

Multiply the numerator factors together and the denominator factors together.

\[\left(\frac54\right)^{3}=\frac{5^3}{4^3}=\frac{125}{64}\]

Evaluate \(\left(\frac54\right)^{0}\).

The base is nonzero, so its zero power is 1.

\[\left(\frac54\right)^{0}=1\]

Evaluate \(\left(\frac54\right)^{-1}\).

Reverse the fraction and make the exponent positive.

\[\left(\frac54\right)^{-1}=\left(\frac45\right)^{1}=\frac{4}{5}\]

Evaluate \(\left(\frac54\right)^{-2}\).

Reverse the fraction and make the exponent positive.

\[\left(\frac54\right)^{-2}=\left(\frac45\right)^{2}=\frac{16}{25}\]

Evaluate \(\left(\frac54\right)^{-3}\).

Reverse the fraction and make the exponent positive.

\[\left(\frac54\right)^{-3}=\left(\frac45\right)^{3}=\frac{64}{125}\]

Principal square roots

Squaring is raising a number to the second power. Finding a square root reverses that operation. The principal square root of a nonnegative number is the nonnegative number whose square is the original number.

Perfect squares

Useful perfect squares include \(0,\allowbreak 1,\allowbreak 4,\allowbreak 9,\allowbreak 16,\allowbreak 25,\allowbreak 36,\allowbreak 49,\allowbreak 64,\allowbreak 81,\allowbreak 100,\allowbreak 121,\allowbreak 144,\allowbreak 169\). Learning these values makes later work with radicals and the quadratic formula faster.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{36}\).

Since \(6^2=36\),

\[\sqrt{36}=6.\]

The radical symbol asks for the principal square root, so the answer is \(6\), not \(\pm6\). Although \((-6)^2=36\) too, \(-6\) is not the principal square root.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{81}\).

When the radicand is a perfect square, find the nonnegative number that squares to it. Since \(9^2=81\),

\[\sqrt{81}=9.\]

Negative radicands: real or complex answers?

No real number has a negative square. If the problem or situation allows only real numbers, a square root of a negative number has no real value. Saying “there is no real square root” is then a complete answer; we do not need to continue.

In other contexts, imaginary numbers are useful and complex answers are allowed. We then use the imaginary unit \(i\), with \(i^2=-1\) and principal square root \(\sqrt{-1}=i\). Separate the negative radicand into a positive factor and \(-1\), then write the result in terms of \(i\).

This choice matters in applications as well as in math class: decide which kinds of numbers are meaningful for the question. A real-only model may rule out an answer, while another model or assignment may call for complex numbers. “No real value” does not mean “no complex value.” The next two problems explicitly request answers in terms of \(i\), so we continue beyond the real numbers.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{-25}\), writing the answer in terms of \(i\).

There is no real square root. Here the directions request an answer in terms of \(i\), so use complex numbers.

\[\sqrt{-25}=\sqrt{25}\sqrt{-1}=5i.\]

The answer is not \(-5\): \((-5)^2=25\), not \(-25\). Instead, \((5i)^2=25i^2=-25\).

Evaluate \(\sqrt{-121}\), writing the answer in terms of \(i\).

There is no real square root. Here the directions request an answer in terms of \(i\), so use complex numbers.

Since \(11^2=121\), the square root of the positive factor is \(11\).

\[\sqrt{-121}=\sqrt{121}\sqrt{-1}=11i.\]

Check by squaring: \((11i)^2=121i^2=-121\).

Compare and contrast the following two problems. Evaluate \(\sqrt{9}\), and solve \(x^2=9\). Explain why the number of answers differs.

Evaluating a radical asks for the principal square root, so

\[\sqrt{9}=3.\]

Solving the equation asks for every value whose square is \(9\). Both \(3^2=9\) and \((-3)^2=9\), so

\[x=3\quad\text{or}\quad x=-3,\qquad\text{equivalently }x=\pm3.\]

The radical has one principal value; the equation has two solutions.
