Topic 5: Evaluating Square Roots

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic evaluates square roots when the radicand is a perfect square and distinguishes real square roots from square roots of negative numbers.

Key definitions

Square root: a number that produces the radicand when multiplied by itself. Radicand: the expression beneath a radical sign. Perfect square: the square of an integer. Imaginary unit: i, defined by i squared equals negative 1.

Principal square roots

A square root reverses squaring. The principal square root of a nonnegative number is the nonnegative number whose square is the original number.

Perfect squares

Useful perfect squares include \(0,\allowbreak 1,\allowbreak 4,\allowbreak 9,\allowbreak 16,\allowbreak 25,\allowbreak 36,\allowbreak 49,\allowbreak 64,\allowbreak 81,\allowbreak 100,\allowbreak 121,\allowbreak 144,\allowbreak 169\). Learning these values makes later work with radicals and the quadratic formula faster.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{36}\).

Since \(6^2=36\),

\[\sqrt{36}=6.\]

The radical symbol asks for the principal square root, so the answer is \(6\), not \(\pm6\). Although \((-6)^2=36\) too, \(-6\) is not the principal square root.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{81}\).

When the radicand is a perfect square, find the nonnegative number that squares to it. Since \(9^2=81\),

\[\sqrt{81}=9.\]

Negative radicands: real or complex answers?

No real number has a negative square. If the problem or situation allows only real numbers, a square root of a negative number has no real value. Saying “there is no real square root” is then a complete answer; we do not need to continue.

In other contexts, imaginary numbers are useful and complex answers are allowed. We then use the imaginary unit \(i\), with \(i^2=-1\) and principal square root \(\sqrt{-1}=i\). Separate the negative radicand into a positive factor and \(-1\), then write the result in terms of \(i\).

This choice matters in applications as well as in math class: decide which kinds of numbers are meaningful for the question. A real-only model may rule out an answer, while another model or assignment may call for complex numbers. “No real value” does not mean “no complex value.” The next two problems explicitly request answers in terms of \(i\), so we continue beyond the real numbers.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{-25}\), writing the answer in terms of \(i\).

There is no real square root. Here the directions request an answer in terms of \(i\), so use complex numbers.

\[\sqrt{-25}=\sqrt{25}\sqrt{-1}=5i.\]

The answer is not \(-5\): \((-5)^2=25\), not \(-25\). Instead, \((5i)^2=25i^2=-25\).

Evaluate \(\sqrt{-121}\), writing the answer in terms of \(i\).

There is no real square root. Here the directions request an answer in terms of \(i\), so use complex numbers.

Since \(11^2=121\), the square root of the positive factor is \(11\).

\[\sqrt{-121}=\sqrt{121}\sqrt{-1}=11i.\]

Check by squaring: \((11i)^2=121i^2=-121\).

Compare and contrast the following two problems. Evaluate \(\sqrt{9}\), and solve \(x^2=9\). Explain why the number of answers differs.

Evaluating a radical asks for the principal square root, so

\[\sqrt{9}=3.\]

Solving the equation asks for every value whose square is \(9\). Both \(3^2=9\) and \((-3)^2=9\), so

\[x=3\quad\text{or}\quad x=-3,\qquad\text{equivalently }x=\pm3.\]

The radical has one principal value; the equation has two solutions.
