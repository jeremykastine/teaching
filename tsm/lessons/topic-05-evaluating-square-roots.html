Topic 5: Evaluating Powers and Square Roots

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic evaluates powers with positive, zero, and negative integer exponents, including negative bases. It then connects squaring with square roots, evaluates roots of perfect squares, and distinguishes real and complex answers.

Key definitions

Base: the quantity being raised to a power. Exponent: for a positive integer exponent, the number of copies of the base used as factors. Power: an expression consisting of a base raised to an exponent. A zero exponent on a nonzero base gives 1; a negative integer exponent gives a reciprocal. Square root: a number that produces the radicand when multiplied by itself. Radicand: the expression beneath a radical sign. Perfect square: the square of an integer. Imaginary unit: i, defined by i squared equals negative 1.

Reading powers

For a positive integer exponent, the base is the repeated factor and the exponent counts its copies. An exponent is not a multiplier of the base. To connect positive, zero, and negative integer exponents, start from 1: multiply by the base for each positive step, do nothing for exponent zero, and divide by the base for each negative step. Zero and negative exponents require a nonzero base.

As the exponent decreases by one, divide the preceding value by the base. Recall from Topic 1: Division and Divisibility that successive divisions combine into a single division by the product of their divisors, and division by a product can be separated into successive divisions.

  1. \(2^6\)
  2. \(7^0\)

    \[6^1=1\cdot6=6\]

Signs, parentheses, and negative exponents

Parentheses determine whether a negative sign is part of the base. With parentheses, the exponent applies to the entire negative base. Without parentheses, evaluate the power of the positive base first, then apply the outside minus sign. A negative base has a positive value for an even integer exponent and a negative value for an odd integer exponent. A nonzero base to the zero power equals 1. A negative exponent indicates division, not a negative value: \(a^{-n}=1/a^n\) for \(a\ne0\).

The answers agree for odd exponents (3, 1, and \(-1\)). For even exponents (2, 0, and \(-2\)), parentheses give a positive answer while the outside minus sign gives a negative answer. Exponent zero gives 1 for either nonzero base, but an outside minus sign still changes that 1 to \(-1\).

For negative exponents, successive divisions still combine by multiplying their divisors. For example, two divisions by \(-5\) combine into division by 25. The negative exponent tells us to divide; the placement of the minus sign determines the answer's sign.

Principal square roots

Squaring is raising a number to the second power. Finding a square root reverses that operation. The principal square root of a nonnegative number is the nonnegative number whose square is the original number.

Perfect squares

Useful perfect squares include \(0,\allowbreak 1,\allowbreak 4,\allowbreak 9,\allowbreak 16,\allowbreak 25,\allowbreak 36,\allowbreak 49,\allowbreak 64,\allowbreak 81,\allowbreak 100,\allowbreak 121,\allowbreak 144,\allowbreak 169\). Learning these values makes later work with radicals and the quadratic formula faster.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{36}\).

Since \(6^2=36\),

\[\sqrt{36}=6.\]

The radical symbol asks for the principal square root, so the answer is \(6\), not \(\pm6\). Although \((-6)^2=36\) too, \(-6\) is not the principal square root.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{81}\).

When the radicand is a perfect square, find the nonnegative number that squares to it. Since \(9^2=81\),

\[\sqrt{81}=9.\]

Negative radicands: real or complex answers?

No real number has a negative square. If the problem or situation allows only real numbers, a square root of a negative number has no real value. Saying “there is no real square root” is then a complete answer; we do not need to continue.

In other contexts, imaginary numbers are useful and complex answers are allowed. We then use the imaginary unit \(i\), with \(i^2=-1\) and principal square root \(\sqrt{-1}=i\). Separate the negative radicand into a positive factor and \(-1\), then write the result in terms of \(i\).

This choice matters in applications as well as in math class: decide which kinds of numbers are meaningful for the question. A real-only model may rule out an answer, while another model or assignment may call for complex numbers. “No real value” does not mean “no complex value.” The next two problems explicitly request answers in terms of \(i\), so we continue beyond the real numbers.

Evaluate \(\sqrt{-25}\), writing the answer in terms of \(i\).

There is no real square root. Here the directions request an answer in terms of \(i\), so use complex numbers.

\[\sqrt{-25}=\sqrt{25}\sqrt{-1}=5i.\]

The answer is not \(-5\): \((-5)^2=25\), not \(-25\). Instead, \((5i)^2=25i^2=-25\).

Evaluate \(\sqrt{-121}\), writing the answer in terms of \(i\).

There is no real square root. Here the directions request an answer in terms of \(i\), so use complex numbers.

Since \(11^2=121\), the square root of the positive factor is \(11\).

\[\sqrt{-121}=\sqrt{121}\sqrt{-1}=11i.\]

Check by squaring: \((11i)^2=121i^2=-121\).

Compare and contrast the following two problems. Evaluate \(\sqrt{9}\), and solve \(x^2=9\). Explain why the number of answers differs.

Evaluating a radical asks for the principal square root, so

\[\sqrt{9}=3.\]

Solving the equation asks for every value whose square is \(9\). Both \(3^2=9\) and \((-3)^2=9\), so

\[x=3\quad\text{or}\quad x=-3,\qquad\text{equivalently }x=\pm3.\]

The radical has one principal value; the equation has two solutions.
