Topic 13: Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic finds the largest whole number that divides every number in a set, using factor lists or prime factorizations.

Key definitions

Common divisor: a whole number that divides each given number. Greatest common divisor (GCD), also called greatest common factor (GCF): the largest common divisor.

Find the GCF of 12 and 20 by listing their factors.

The positive factors of twelve are \(1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 6,\ 12\). The positive factors of twenty are \(1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 5,\ 10,\ 20\). Their common factors are \(1,\ 2,\ 4\), and the greatest of these is four.

\[GCF(12,\ 20)\ = \ 4\]

A factor and a divisor describe the same relationship in this setting: each divides into the number with no remainder. Some materials use the abbreviation GCF for greatest common factor, while others use GCD for greatest common divisor. The two names ask for the same answer.

The GCF or GCD is the largest positive factor shared by every number in the problem. For an LCM, we collected enough prime factors to cover all of the numbers. For a GCF, we keep only prime factors that all of the numbers have in common.

Find the GCD of 12 and 20 using prime factorization.

The prime factorization of twelve is:

\[12\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\ \cdot \ 3\]

The prime factorization of twenty is:

\[20\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\ \cdot \ 5\]

Both numbers contain two copies of two. The three appears only in twelve, and the five appears only in twenty, so neither belongs in the common factor.

\[GCF(12,\ 20)\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\]

\[GCD(12,20)=2\cdot2=4.\]

Find the GCD of 50, 60, and 70 using prime factorization.

\[50=2\cdot5\cdot5\]

The second number has these prime factors.

\[60\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\ \cdot \ 3\ \cdot \ 5\]

The third number has these prime factors.

\[70\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 5\ \cdot \ 7\]

Every number contains one copy of two and one copy of five. No additional prime factor appears in all three numbers.

\[GCD(50,\ 60,\ 70)\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 5\]

Evaluate the shared product.

\[GCD(50,\ 60,\ 70)\ = \ 10\]

Find the GCD of 6, 10, and 15 using prime factorization.

\[6=2\cdot3\]

The second number is:

\[10\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 5\]

The third number is:

\[15\ = \ 3\ \cdot \ 5\]

Each pair of numbers shares a prime factor, but no prime factor appears in all three factorizations. A common factor must belong to every number, not merely to some pair of numbers. The only positive factor shared by all three is one.

\[GCF(6,\ 10,\ 15)\ = \ 1\]

Find the GCD of 120 and 94 using prime factorization.

\[120=2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot3\cdot5\]

Ninety-four is two times forty-seven, and forty-seven is prime.

\[94\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 47\]

The two numbers share one copy of two. The extra twos in 120 cannot be used because 94 does not contain them, and none of the other prime factors are shared.

\[GCD(120,\ 94)\ = \ 2\]

Prime factorization reveals the structure underneath each whole number. For an LCM, collect enough copies of each prime to cover every number. For a GCF or GCD, keep only the copies that every number shares. The phrase “cover every number” points toward the LCM; the phrase “shared by every number” points toward the GCF.
