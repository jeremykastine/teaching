Topic 22: Adding and Subtracting Fractions

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic adds and subtracts fractions by creating a common denominator, combining numerators, and simplifying the result when possible.

Key definitions

Common denominator: a shared denominator for two or more fractions. Least common denominator (LCD): the least common multiple of the denominators. Equivalent fraction: a fraction with the same value but a different numerator and denominator.

Add and simplify.

\(\frac{3}{10}\ + \ \frac{1}{10}\)

Both terms are measured in tenths. The three tenths and the one tenth work together, so add the numerator counts. The denominator remains ten because the pieces are still tenths.

\(\frac{4}{10}\)

The numerator and denominator share a factor of two. Divide both by two to write the answer in lowest terms.

\(\frac{2}{5}\)

When fractions are added or subtracted, the denominator tells us what kind of pieces are being counted. The numerator tells us how many of those pieces we have. Tenths can be combined with tenths, fifteenths with fifteenths, and so on. When the denominators are already the same, the fractions are already written using the same kind of piece.

The denominator is not another quantity to add or subtract. It names the size of the pieces. Once the pieces have the same name, work only with the numerators and keep the common denominator. Afterward, check whether the resulting fraction can be reduced.

When the denominators are different, the fractions cannot yet be combined. First give the fractions new, equivalent names with a common denominator. The least common multiple of the denominators is usually the best choice because it keeps the numbers as small as possible. As in equivalent-fraction work, every change is made by multiplying a numerator and its denominator by the same number, which is multiplication by one.

The ideas about positive and negative terms still apply. In an addition problem, positive terms work together. In a subtraction problem, the subtracted fraction is a negative term. Once the denominators match, decide which terms are working together or against each other, which side wins, and by how much.

Subtract and simplify.

\(\frac{22}{15}\ - \ \frac{8}{15}\)

Both terms are measured in fifteenths. The first term is positive and the second term is negative, so the terms are working against each other. The positive term has twenty-two fifteenths and the negative term has eight fifteenths. The positive side wins by fourteen fifteenths.

\(\frac{14}{15}\)

Fourteen and fifteen have no common factor greater than one, so the fraction is already in lowest terms.

Add and simplify.

\(\frac{4}{16}\ + \ \frac{3}{16}\ + \ \frac{2}{16}\)

All three terms are positive and all three are measured in sixteenths. The terms work together. Add the numerators four, three, and two, and keep the denominator sixteen.

\(\frac{9}{16}\)

Nine and sixteen share no common factor greater than one, so no reduction is needed.

Add and simplify.

\(\frac{2}{3}\ + \ \frac{1}{6}\)

Thirds and sixths are different kinds of pieces. Six is already a multiple of three, so use six as the common denominator. Multiply two thirds by two over two. The second fraction can remain as it is.

\(\frac{2}{3}\ \cdot \ \frac{2}{2}\ + \ \frac{1}{6}\)

The first fraction is now written in sixths.

\(\frac{4}{6}\ + \ \frac{1}{6}\)

The four sixths and one sixth work together.

\(\frac{5}{6}\)

Add and simplify.

\(\frac{5}{27}\ + \ \frac{1}{6}\)

Neither denominator is a multiple of the other. The least common multiple of twenty-seven and six is fifty-four. Multiply five twenty-sevenths by two over two, and multiply one sixth by nine over nine.

\(\frac{5}{27}\ \cdot \ \frac{2}{2}\ + \ \frac{1}{6}\ \cdot \ \frac{9}{9}\)

Both fractions are now written in fifty-fourths.

\(\frac{10}{54}\ + \ \frac{9}{54}\)

The positive terms work together. Add the numerators and keep the common denominator.

\(\frac{19}{54}\)

Nineteen and fifty-four have no common factor greater than one.

Add and simplify.

\(\frac{7}{27}\ + \ \frac{7}{45}\ + \ \frac{3}{10}\)

For several denominators, prime factorization can make the least common multiple easier to see. Twenty-seven contains three factors of three. Forty-five contains two factors of three and one factor of five. Ten contains one factor of two and one factor of five. To cover all three denominators, the least common denominator must contain one factor of two, three factors of three, and one factor of five. That denominator is two hundred seventy.

To change twenty-seven into two hundred seventy, multiply by ten. To change forty-five into two hundred seventy, multiply by six. To change ten into two hundred seventy, multiply by twenty-seven. Use the same multipliers in the numerators.

\(\frac{7}{27}\ \cdot \ \frac{10}{10}\ + \ \frac{7}{45}\ \cdot \ \frac{6}{6}\ + \ \frac{3}{10}\ \cdot \ \frac{27}{27}\)

Each fraction is now written in two-hundred-seventieths.

\(\frac{70}{270}\ + \ \frac{42}{270}\ + \ \frac{81}{270}\)

All three terms are positive, so the numerator counts work together. Seventy plus forty-two plus eighty-one is one hundred ninety-three.

\(\frac{193}{270}\)

One hundred ninety-three and two hundred seventy have no common factor greater than one.

Subtract and simplify.

\(\frac{4}{5}\ - \ \frac{3}{10}\)

Use ten as the common denominator. Multiply four fifths by two over two. The second fraction can remain unchanged.

\(\frac{4}{5}\ \cdot \ \frac{2}{2}\ - \ \frac{3}{10}\)

The fractions are now both written in tenths.

\(\frac{8}{10}\ - \ \frac{3}{10}\)

The positive term has eight tenths and the negative term has three tenths. The positive side wins by five tenths.

\(\frac{5}{10}\)

Five and ten share a factor of five. Divide both by five.

\(\frac{1}{2}\)

Subtract and simplify.

\(\frac{2}{3}\ - \ \frac{1}{5}\)

The least common multiple of three and five is fifteen. Multiply two thirds by five over five, and multiply one fifth by three over three.

\(\frac{2}{3}\ \cdot \ \frac{5}{5}\ - \ \frac{1}{5}\ \cdot \ \frac{3}{3}\)

Both terms are now written in fifteenths.

\(\frac{10}{15}\ - \ \frac{3}{15}\)

The positive term has ten fifteenths and the negative term has three fifteenths. The positive side wins by seven fifteenths.

\(\frac{7}{15}\)

Evaluate. Express the answer as an improper fraction in lowest terms.

\(3\ + \ \frac{3}{5}\)

A whole number can be written as a fraction with denominator one. This makes the whole number an ordinary fraction that can be given a common denominator.

\(\frac{3}{1}\ + \ \frac{3}{5}\)

Use five as the common denominator. Multiply three over one by five over five.

\(\frac{3}{1}\ \cdot \ \frac{5}{5}\ + \ \frac{3}{5}\)

Both terms are now written in fifths.

\(\frac{15}{5}\ + \ \frac{3}{5}\)

The terms work together.

\(\frac{18}{5}\)

The requested answer is an improper fraction, so leave the result as eighteen fifths rather than changing it to a mixed number.

Evaluate. Express the answer as an improper fraction in lowest terms.

\(2\ - \ \frac{1}{7}\)

Write the whole number as a fraction with denominator one.

\(\frac{2}{1}\ - \ \frac{1}{7}\)

Use seven as the common denominator. Multiply two over one by seven over seven.

\(\frac{2}{1}\ \cdot \ \frac{7}{7}\ - \ \frac{1}{7}\)

Both terms are now written in sevenths.

\(\frac{14}{7}\ - \ \frac{1}{7}\)

The positive term has fourteen sevenths and the negative term has one seventh. The positive side wins by thirteen sevenths.

\(\frac{13}{7}\)

To add or subtract fractions, first make sure every term is written using the same denominator. If the denominators already match, work with the numerators and keep the denominator. If they do not match, use equivalent fractions to create a common denominator, preferably the least common multiple. Keep every displayed step equivalent to the original expression, simplify the final fraction, and leave an answer improper when the directions request an improper fraction.
