Topic 6: Simplifying Square Roots

Assigned to: TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic explores decimal approximations of irrational square roots, then rewrites square roots in simplest radical form by separating perfect-square factors. Simplification gives exact expressions and can reuse known square-root approximations.

Key definitions

Simplest radical form: no perfect-square factor greater than 1 remains under the radical. Perfect-square factor: a factor such as 4, 9, 16, or 25 that has an integer square root. Irrational number: a real number that cannot be written as a ratio of two integers; its decimal expansion neither terminates nor eventually repeats. Approximation: a value close to the exact number.

Locate \(\sqrt{40}\) between consecutive integers by squaring trial values. Refine your lower and upper bounds using tenths, hundredths, thousandths, and ten-thousandths. You may use a four-function calculator, but do not use a square-root key. Explain what happens if you keep adding decimal places.

We want the nonnegative number whose square is 40. Test a number by multiplying it by itself. A square below 40 means the guess is too small; a square above 40 means it is too large.

Try nearby integers:

\[6^2=36<40\]

\[7^2=49>40\]

\[6<\sqrt{40}<7\]

Try nearby tenths:

\[6.3^2=39.69<40\]

\[6.4^2=40.96>40\]

\[6.3<\sqrt{40}<6.4\]

Try nearby hundredths:

\[6.32^2=39.9424<40\]

\[6.33^2=40.0689>40\]

\[6.32<\sqrt{40}<6.33\]

Try nearby thousandths:

\[6.324^2=39.992976<40\]

\[6.325^2=40.005625>40\]

\[6.324<\sqrt{40}<6.325\]

Try nearby ten-thousandths:

\[6.3245^2=39.99930025<40\]

\[6.3246^2=40.00056516>40\]

\[6.3245<\sqrt{40}<6.3246\]

The intervals become narrower as we add decimal places. We can keep going beyond the tenths, hundredths, thousandths, and ten-thousandths. Each stage gives a closer approximation; no finite number of decimal places gives the exact value.

Why does this process never finish? Mathematicians have proved that the square root of a positive integer that is not a perfect square is an irrational number. Since 40 is not a perfect square, \(\sqrt{40}\) is irrational: it cannot be written as a ratio of two integers. Its decimal expansion never terminates and never settles into a fixed repeating block. The proof of the non-repeating property is beyond this course; the trials above illustrate approximation rather than proving irrationality.

\[\sqrt{40}=6.324555320\ldots\]

We can get as close as we wish, even though no finite decimal is exactly right. Writing \(\sqrt{40}\) already names the number exactly; using a decimal such as \(6.3246\) names an approximation.

A short square-root table gives \(\sqrt{10}\approx3.16228\) but has no entry for \(\sqrt{40}\). Rewrite \(\sqrt{40}\) in terms of \(\sqrt{10}\), then use the table entry to estimate it. Identify your exact result and your decimal approximation.

Forty is \(4\cdot10\), and 4 is a perfect square. Separate the perfect-square factor:

\[\sqrt{40}=\sqrt{4\cdot10}\]

\[\sqrt{40}=\sqrt{4}\sqrt{10}=2\sqrt{10}\]

For nonnegative real numbers \(a\) and \(b\), the product property is \(\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}\). Here it lets us reuse the square root of 10 rather than calculate the square root of 40 from scratch.

Using the supplied table entry gives:

\[\sqrt{40}\approx2(3.16228)=6.32456\]

The equality \(\sqrt{40}=2\sqrt{10}\) is exact. The decimal \(6.32456\) is approximate because the table entry is rounded. Simplifying the radical does not make its decimal terminate; it gives a simpler exact name for the same number.

Historical square-root tables were real tools for saving calculation. Peter Barlow's 1814 New Mathematical Tables included square roots of integers from 1 to 10,000. A 1911 account of mathematical tables explicitly explains their value in saving time and labor, noting the work needed to calculate square roots by hand.

Our short-table example illustrates how simplifying a radical can help reuse a table: a known root can supply an approximation for another root. Building and checking a table took work; reusing its entries avoided repeating that work.

Simplify \(\sqrt{75}\). Identify the perfect-square factor you use.

The largest perfect-square factor of \(75\) is \(25\). Write the radicand as a product:

\[\sqrt{75}=\sqrt{25\cdot3}\]

Use the product property for nonnegative factors, then evaluate the perfect-square part:

\[\sqrt{75}=\sqrt{25}\sqrt{3}=5\sqrt{3}\]

\(3\) has no perfect-square factor greater than 1, so \(5\sqrt{3}\) is in simplest radical form. The remaining radicand is called squarefree.

Simplify \(\sqrt{90}\). Identify the perfect-square factor you use.

The largest perfect-square factor of \(90\) is \(9\). Write the radicand as a product:

\[\sqrt{90}=\sqrt{9\cdot10}\]

Use the product property for nonnegative factors, then evaluate the perfect-square part:

\[\sqrt{90}=\sqrt{9}\sqrt{10}=3\sqrt{10}\]

\(10\) has no perfect-square factor greater than 1, so \(3\sqrt{10}\) is in simplest radical form. Choosing the largest perfect-square factor when possible avoids extra reduction steps.

Simplify \(\sqrt{-45}\) in terms of \(i\). Identify the perfect-square factor you use.

First separate the negative sign using \(i=\sqrt{-1}\). Then simplify the positive radical; its largest perfect-square factor is \(9\).

\[\sqrt{-45}=i\sqrt{45}\]

\[i\sqrt{45}=i\sqrt{9\cdot5}\]

\[i\sqrt{9}\sqrt{5}=3i\sqrt{5}\]

The result is \(3i\sqrt{5}\). The remaining radicand \(5\) has no perfect-square factor greater than 1.

A negative radicand is different from a negative sign outside the radical: \(\sqrt{-45}\ne-\sqrt{45}\). The first is imaginary; the second is a negative real number.

Simplify \(\sqrt{-80}\) in terms of \(i\). Identify the perfect-square factor you use.

First separate the negative sign using \(i=\sqrt{-1}\). Then simplify the positive radical; its largest perfect-square factor is \(16\).

\[\sqrt{-80}=i\sqrt{80}\]

\[i\sqrt{80}=i\sqrt{16\cdot5}\]

\[i\sqrt{16}\sqrt{5}=4i\sqrt{5}\]

The result is \(4i\sqrt{5}\). The remaining radicand \(5\) has no perfect-square factor greater than 1.

Check by squaring: \((4i\sqrt{5})^2=16i^2\cdot5=-80\).
