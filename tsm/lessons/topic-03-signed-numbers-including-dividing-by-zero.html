Topic 3: Signed Numbers, Including Dividing by Zero

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Overview

This topic interprets and performs addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division with positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero.

Key definitions

Signed number: a positive number, negative number, or zero. Opposites: numbers the same distance from 0 with different signs. Undefined: an expression that has no numerical value; division by 0 is undefined.

\[10\]

Before trying to calculate an addition or subtraction expression, first learn to read its terms. A term is a number together with the addition or subtraction attached to it. The first term is a special case: when no sign is written before it, the term is understood to be positive.

The sign of a term tells us the effect that the entire term has on the expression. To decide whether a term is positive or negative, look at all of the signs attached to that term. Do not look only at the symbol between the two numbers.

\[3\ + \ 7\]

The first term is positive three. Nothing is written before the three, so a positive sign is understood. The second term is positive seven because seven is being added.

\[3\ - \ 7\]

The first term is positive three. The second term is negative seven because the subtraction sign is attached to the seven. The term has a negative effect even though the number 7 is written without a negative sign of its own.

\[3\ + \ ( - 7)\]

The first term is positive three. The second term is negative seven. The plus sign tells us that a number is being added, but the number being added is negative. We accept the term exactly as it is written; there is no need to rewrite it as subtraction.

\[- 6\ + \ 8\]

The first term is negative six because a negative sign is written directly in front of the first number. The second term is positive eight because eight is being added.

\[- 6\ - \ 8\]

The first term is negative six. The second term is also negative because eight is being subtracted. Both terms therefore have a negative effect.

\[- 6\ - \ ( - 8)\]

The first term is negative six. The second term is positive eight because the subtraction sign and the negative sign are both attached to the same eight. This is the useful double-negative situation. The second term may optionally be rewritten as positive eight, but the rewrite is not required in order to recognize the sign of the term.

Once the signs of the terms are clear, decide whether the terms are on the same team or on competing teams. Two positive terms work together for the positive team. Two negative terms work together for the negative team. A positive term and a negative term compete against each other. When the teams compete, ask: Which team wins, and by how much?

This way of thinking lets us work with the terms exactly as they are written. A term written as adding a negative number stays written that way. A term written as subtracting a positive number also stays written that way. The only rewrite that may be useful is the optional rewrite of a double negative.

The central habit is to identify the sign of each entire term before doing any arithmetic. Terms on the same team work together. Terms on opposite teams compete, and the answer takes the sign of the team that wins. There is no need to convert every addition problem into subtraction or every subtraction problem into addition. Read the terms as they are written.

The sign rule for multiplication and division should work just as well when an expression contains three, four, or five factors as it does when it contains only two. Deal with negative signs in pairs.

Within multiplication and division, any two negative signs may be paired together, and that pair has a positive effect. If a negative sign is left without a partner, that single negative carries forward into the next step and eventually into the answer. With a longer expression, continue pairing negatives until either none remain or one remains.

This rule belongs specifically to multiplication and division. Two separate negative terms in addition or subtraction do not become positive merely because two negative signs are visible.

It is still useful to separate the sign decision from the basic arithmetic. After pairing the negative signs, multiply or divide the sizes of the numbers. Strong multiplication facts make this much faster and become especially important later when factoring.

For example, forty-nine can be recognized as a product without using a calculator.

Zero may be divided by any nonzero number.

\[0\ \div \ ( - 53)\]

The answer is zero. The single negative does not turn zero into a negative number; zero has no positive or negative size to carry. The quotient is still zero.

\[0\]

The multiplication check is \(0\ \cdot \ ( - 53)\ = \ 0\).

Determine whether the expression is defined. If it is, evaluate it.

\[( - 7)\ \div \ 0\]

Division by zero is a different situation.

\[( - 7)\ \div \ 0\]

A quotient would have to be a number that becomes negative seven when multiplied by zero. But every expression of the form \(?\ \cdot \ 0\) has a value of zero. No number can make the multiplication check return negative seven.

Therefore, this division has no defined value.

\[undefined\]

Keep the two zero facts separate: zero divided by a nonzero number is zero, while a nonzero number divided by zero is undefined. For signs in multiplication and division, pair negatives. Every pair has a positive effect, and any single negative left over carries forward.
