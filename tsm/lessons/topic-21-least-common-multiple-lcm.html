Topic 21: Least Common Multiple (LCM)

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

This topic finds the smallest positive whole number that is a multiple of every number in a set.

Key definitions

Multiple: a product of a number and a whole number. Common multiple: a number that is a multiple of each given number. Least common multiple (LCM): the smallest positive common multiple.

Find the LCM of 6 and 8 by listing their multiples.

The positive multiples of six begin \(6,\ 12,\ 18,\ 24,\ 30,\ \ldots\). The positive multiples of eight begin \(8,\ 16,\ 24,\ 32,\ \ldots\). The first number appearing in both lists is twenty-four.

\[LCM(6,\ 8)\ = \ 24\]

A multiple of a number is obtained by multiplying that number by a whole number. The least common multiple, or LCM, is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of every number in the problem. It is the first positive amount at which all of the multiplication patterns meet.

For small numbers, listing multiples may be the quickest method. For larger numbers, prime factorizations show exactly which prime factors must be included.

Find the LCM of 6 and 8 using prime factorization.

The prime factorization of six is:

\[6\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 3\]

The prime factorization of eight is:

\[8\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\]

The LCM must contain enough prime factors to cover both original numbers. Three copies of two are needed to cover eight, and one copy of three is needed to cover six. We do not add another two for six because the three copies already include the one copy that six needs.

\[LCM(6,\ 8)\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\ \cdot \ 3\]

\[LCM(6,8)=2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot3=24.\]

Find the LCM of 4, 5, and 6 using prime factorization.

\[4=2\cdot2\]

The second number is already prime.

\[5\ = \ 5\]

The final number has two different prime factors.

\[6\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 3\]

To cover four, the LCM needs two copies of two. Those two copies already cover the single two needed by six. The factors three and five must also be included.

\[LCM(4,\ 5,\ 6)\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 2\ \cdot \ 3\ \cdot \ 5\]

Multiply the selected factors.

\[LCM(4,\ 5,\ 6)\ = \ 60\]

Find the LCM of 16 and 20 using prime factorization.

\[16=2^4\]

Twenty contains two copies of two and one copy of five.

\[20\ = \ 2^{2}\ \cdot \ 5\]

Four copies of two cover all of the twos needed by both numbers. Only the factor five must be added. Multiplying the complete factorizations together would count shared factors more than necessary and would produce a common multiple, but not the least one.

\[LCM(16,\ 20)\ = \ 2^{4}\ \cdot \ 5\]

Evaluate the product.

\[LCM(16,\ 20)\ = \ 80\]

Find the LCM of 10 and 21 using prime factorization.

The prime factorizations have no prime factors in common.

\[10\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 5\]

The second factorization is:

\[21\ = \ 3\ \cdot \ 7\]

Since there is no overlap, every prime factor from both numbers is needed.

\[LCM(10,\ 21)\ = \ 2\ \cdot \ 3\ \cdot \ 5\ \cdot \ 7\]

\[LCM(10,21)=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7=210.\]
