Topic 15: Factoring by Guess and Check

Assigned to: TSM 098 and TSM 099

Double-click or double-tap the space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Overview

Factor by guessing possible binomial factors and multiplying to check them. Use a decision tree, then a table, to keep track of the choices. Include leading coefficients greater than 1 and differences of squares.

Key definitions

Factor: an expression being multiplied. Trinomial: a polynomial with three terms. Leading coefficient: the coefficient of the highest-degree term. Factor pair: two numbers or terms with a specified product. Prime polynomial: a nonconstant polynomial that cannot be factored into nonunit polynomial factors over the integers. Difference of squares: one squared expression minus another.

Guess the factors; check by multiplying

Factoring reverses multiplication. First check for a GCF. For a quadratic, try two binomials: their first terms must multiply to the leading term, and their last terms must multiply to the constant term. Those choices produce a guess. Multiply to see whether its middle terms combine correctly. Until a guess passes the check, do not write it as equal to the original polynomial.

Factor completely over the integers. Guess two binomial factors and check by multiplying.

\[t^2+9t+8\]

The first terms can be \(t\) and \(t\). For the last terms, try \(+8\) and \(+1\), whose product is 8. Our guess is \((t+8)(t+1)\). Multiply to check:

\[(t+8)(t+1)=t^2+t+8t+8=t^2+9t+8\]

All three terms match, so the guess works.

\[t^2+9t+8=(t+8)(t+1)\]

Factor completely over the integers. Show an unsuccessful guess before finding and checking a successful guess.

\[s^2+8s+15\]

The first terms can be \(s\) and \(s\). The last terms must multiply to 15. Try \((s+1)(s+15)\). Its product is \(s^2+16s+15\), so reject it: the middle term is wrong.

Try another guess, \((s+3)(s+5)\).

\[(s+3)(s+5)=s^2+5s+3s+15=s^2+8s+15\]

\[s^2+8s+15=(s+3)(s+5)\]

Organize the decisions in a tree

Choose the first terms, then choose the last terms and their placement. Each endpoint of the tree represents a guess to check. For a positive leading coefficient, we can use positive first terms: two negative first terms can be replaced by positive ones by negating both entire factors. List each first-term pair once, and allow the different last-term placements. Interchanging two whole factors does not change a product; exchanging only the last terms while keeping the first terms fixed can change it.

Factor completely over the integers. Draw a decision tree of possible binomial factors and check guesses until one works.

\[2x^2+7x+3\]

Use first terms \(x\) and \(2x\). The constant and middle coefficient are positive, so use two positive last terms; two negative last terms would give a negative middle term. The last terms can be placed as \(+1,+3\) or \(+3,+1\).

  • \(2x^2+7x+3\)
    • First terms: \(x,\ 2x\)
      • Last terms: \(+1,\ +3\) → \((x+1)(2x+3)\)
      • Last terms: \(+3,\ +1\) → \((x+3)(2x+1)\)

Try \((x+1)(2x+3)\): the middle terms combine as \(3x+2x=5x\), so reject it. Try \((x+3)(2x+1)\): the middle terms are \(x+6x=7x\).

\[(x+3)(2x+1)=2x^2+x+6x+3=2x^2+7x+3\]

\[2x^2+7x+3=(x+3)(2x+1)\]

The two placements of 1 and 3 give different products. We must include both choices.

Factor completely over the integers. Draw a decision tree including the possible first-term pairs and the different last-term placements.

\[6x^2+7x+2\]

The first terms can be \(x,6x\) or \(2x,3x\). The positive constant and middle coefficient require positive last terms: \(+1,+2\) or \(+2,+1\).

  • \(6x^2+7x+2\)
    • First terms: \(x,\ 6x\)
      • Last terms: \(+1,\ +2\) → \((x+1)(6x+2)\)
      • Last terms: \(+2,\ +1\) → \((x+2)(6x+1)\)
    • First terms: \(2x,\ 3x\)
      • Last terms: \(+1,\ +2\) → \((2x+1)(3x+2)\)
      • Last terms: \(+2,\ +1\) → \((2x+2)(3x+1)\)

Choose any endpoint to test. For example, \((2x+2)(3x+1)\) gives middle terms \(2x+6x=8x\), so cross out that guess. Try \((2x+1)(3x+2)\) next.

\[(2x+1)(3x+2)=6x^2+4x+3x+2=6x^2+7x+2\]

\[6x^2+7x+2=(2x+1)(3x+2)\]

Keep the same choices in a table

A tree can take a lot of space. Put possible first-term pairs down the left and possible unsigned last-term pairs across the top. List each last-term pair once, without adding its reverse as another column. Leave all signs off the table headings, and use just one column for each pair of sizes, regardless of sign choices. Each empty box represents both placements of those sizes.

For a chosen box, keep the first terms in the displayed order. Find the two cross-product sizes, then swap only the last-term sizes and try again. With a positive constant, compare their sum to the size of the middle term; with a negative constant, compare their difference in size. Once a pair works, choose the signs to match the original polynomial and verify by multiplying. Equal first-term sizes or equal last-term sizes can make the two checks identical.

  1. Choose any empty box and put a ? in it.
  2. Find the two cross-product sizes using the first placement, and check their sum or difference.
  3. If it fails, swap the last terms and check the other placement in the same box.
  4. Cross out the question mark only if both placements fail; then choose another empty box.
  5. If either placement works, circle the question mark, choose the signs, and write the successful factorization.

There is no required order for choosing boxes. Leave untried boxes empty. After checking the first and last products, you may check just the middle terms when testing a guess. Verify the final factorization by multiplying. A complete unsuccessful search requires checking both placements in every cell, after making sure no GCF was missed.

Difference of squares: make the middle term zero

A quadratic with no written middle term has a middle coefficient of zero. Use the same guesses and checks, looking for two middle terms that cancel. A difference of squares has the form \(A^2-B^2\), where \(A\) and \(B\) stand for expressions. First obtain the factors by guessing and checking; then observe the cancellation pattern.

Factor completely over the integers by guessing and checking. Show why the missing middle term is zero.

\[x^2-25\]

Think of the original polynomial as \(x^2+0x-25\). First terms \(x,x\) have the required product. Try last terms \(+1,-25\): \((x+1)(x-25)\) gives middle terms \(-25x+x=-24x\), so reject it. Try \(+5,-5\) next.

\[(x+5)(x-5)=x^2-5x+5x-25=x^2-25\]

The middle terms cancel, so the guess works.

\[x^2-25=(x+5)(x-5)\]

Factor completely over the integers by guessing and checking. Verify that the middle terms cancel.

\[9x^2-16\]

The first terms can be \(x,9x\) or \(3x,3x\). Last terms must multiply to -16. Try \((x+4)(9x-4)\): its middle terms combine to \(-4x+36x=32x\), so it fails. Try \((3x+4)(3x-4)\).

\[(3x+4)(3x-4)=9x^2-12x+12x-16=9x^2-16\]

\[9x^2-16=(3x+4)(3x-4)\]

In both examples, equal first terms and opposite last terms made the cross terms cancel. This is the difference-of-squares pattern: \(A^2-B^2=(A+B)(A-B)\). Always check that the original expression is a subtraction of squares.

Factor completely

Check for a GCF before guessing binomial factors. Repeated factors are allowed. Stop only when the remaining factors cannot be factored further over the integers.

Factor completely over the integers. Begin by factoring out the GCF, then guess and check the remaining factors.

\[12x^2+14x+4\]

The GCF is 2. Remove it first:

\[12x^2+14x+4=2(6x^2+7x+2)\]

For the quadratic, try \((x+2)(6x+1)\): its middle term is \(13x\), so reject it. Try \((2x+1)(3x+2)\): the middle terms combine to \(4x+3x=7x\). Keep the outside factor of 2.

\[12x^2+14x+4=2(2x+1)(3x+2)\]

Checking the whole product gives \(2(6x^2+7x+2)=12x^2+14x+4\).

When every guess fails

Failure of a few guesses is not enough to declare a polynomial nonfactorable. Build the complete table, listing each last-term pair once, and check both placements in every box. With a positive constant and positive middle coefficient, positive first terms require positive last terms; two negative last terms would give a negative middle coefficient.
