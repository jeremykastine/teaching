MATH 101 · College Algebra · Winter 2027 planning

Lecture and Assessment Plan

Proposed sequence and test groups · Updated November 10, 2026

Nineteen essential lectures, each designed for one hour, develop College Algebra beginning with general relations. The first example is |x| + |y| = 1: use its diamond-shaped graph to introduce ordered pairs, domain, range, and transformations, then connect these ideas to circles. Next distinguish relations in which y is a function of x: each allowed x determines exactly one y. Continue through concrete families of equations, revisiting graph behavior, transformations, inverses, and equation solving as they arise.

Lectures 1–17 use explicit equations, tables, and graphs. Describe the domain as all x-values occurring in the relation and the range as all y-values occurring in it; these concepts apply before a relation is identified as a function. Discuss where y increases or decreases as x increases, where y is positive or negative, and what happens at endpoints and far along the graph. Introduce general functions and function notation in Lectures 18–19, after students have extensive concrete examples.

Recurring work includes finding all y-values at a supplied x and all x-values at a supplied y, identifying intercepts and extrema, reading average rates of change for functions, matching equations and graphs, transforming a familiar graph, and reversing a relation by exchanging x and y. Distinguish an inverse relation from an inverse that is itself a function. Teach prerequisite algebra and arithmetic where each family needs it.

Four proposed unit tests

  1. General Relations; Linear, Absolute Value, Quadratic, and Square-Root Functions — Lectures 1–5. Read and transform diamonds and circles, identify when y is a function of x, then analyze the concrete function families, their inverses, and associated equations, plus linear and quadratic inequalities.
  2. Power, Polynomial, and Rational Relations — Lectures 6–11. Analyze power and higher-root relations, construct polynomials from graphs, find polynomial zeros, and analyze and solve rational expressions and equations.
  3. Exponential and Logarithmic Relations — Lectures 12–15. Match and analyze graphs, use inverse relationships and logarithmic properties, and solve exact exponential and logarithmic equations.
  4. Systems, Piecewise Relations, and General Functions — Lectures 16–19. Solve systems, interpret piecewise graphs, use function notation, express familiar transformations and inverses in that notation, and form and interpret compositions.

The test assignments are a proposed Winter 2027 grouping. The assessment column uses a test number for each lecture's core problem types. Retain four unit tests designed for 30 minutes, normally five main questions totaling 100 points. Select a manageable sample of the listed skills on each version.

Lecture 1General relations: diamonds, circles, and transformations Begin with |x| + |y| = 1. Build its graph from ordered pairs and the vertices (±1, 0) and (0, ±1); find all y-values for a chosen x. Read domain and range [−1, 1]. Translate horizontally and vertically, and stretch or compress in each direction using concrete equations, leading to |x − h|/a + |y − k|/b = 1, with a, b > 0. Read vertices, domain [h − a, h + a], and range [k − b, k + b]. Horizontal and vertical stretches give distinct changes; reflections of the centered diamond across either axis leave it unchanged. Connect to x2 + y2 = 1 and (x − h)2 + (y − k)2 = r2, with r > 0, using center, radius, intercepts, domain, and range. Review absolute value, signed coordinates, substitution, and interval notation through these examples. Test 1
Lecture 2When relations are functions; linear equations and graphs Use diamonds and circles to show that one x can correspond to multiple y-values. Contrast with a line y = mx + b: y is a function of x when each x in the domain has exactly one y. Connect this definition to the vertical line test and to the upper or lower half of the earlier graphs. Keep function notation for Lecture 18. Evaluate y, solve for x at a supplied y, graph and write lines from points or intercepts, and connect slope to constant and average rates of change. Describe increasing, decreasing, or constant behavior and what happens as x tends toward positive or negative infinity. Apply the earlier transformations to lines. Introduce reversal by exchanging x and y: a nonconstant line has an inverse function, whereas a constant line has a vertical inverse relation. Review fractions in slope and linear equation solving; use related linear inequalities as needed. Test 1
Lecture 3Absolute value equations and functions; quadratic functions Compare y = a|x − h| + k and y = a(x − h)2 + k, with a ≠ 0, emphasizing their shared vertex (h, k), axis of symmetry x = h, upward or downward opening, and minimum or maximum. Contrast the straight arms of the absolute value graph with the curved parabola. Find domain, range, intercepts, and increasing and decreasing intervals; compare end behavior and match transformed equations and graphs. Develop absolute value from distance on the number line, isolate it to solve equations, and interpret two, one, or no solutions as horizontal-line intersections. Relate expanded and vertex forms of a quadratic using the vertex x-coordinate and substitution. Practice writing either function from its vertex and another point. Review signed arithmetic, squaring negative numbers, and expanding binomials as needed. Test 1
Lecture 4Quadratic equations, zeros, and signs Find x where a quadratic has a specified y-value, especially zeros. Choose factoring, taking square roots, or the quadratic formula. Connect √(u2) = |u| to the square-root method. Teach needed GCF and trinomial factoring, fraction reduction, and extraction of perfect-square factors. Use the discriminant to count real zeros and the graph to solve simple quadratic inequalities. Test 1
Lecture 5Square-root relations and inverses of restricted quadratics Reverse a quadratic after restricting it to one side of its vertex. Analyze y = a√(x − h) + k, with a ≠ 0, including domain, range, intercepts, transformations, and behavior at the finite endpoint and along the unbounded direction. Solve a one-radical equation by isolating the radical and squaring. Check candidates in the original equation. Use real radicals and clean exact values. Test 1
Lecture 6Power relations and higher roots Compare even and odd powers and their inverse root relations, including y = x3 and y = ∛x. Study symmetry, allowed inputs and outputs, transformations, and end behavior. Introduce rational exponents through roots and teach exponent laws, including zero and negative exponents, as needed. State nonzero-base conditions for division and nonpositive integer powers. Include simple higher-root equations and the domain restrictions of even roots. Test 2
Lecture 7Polynomial graphs and construction from zeros Read zeros, crossing or touching behavior, multiplicities, and end behavior from a polynomial graph or factored equation. Relate the leading term to degree and end behavior. Construct a degree-3 or degree-4 polynomial from clear zeros and multiplicities, using one supplied point to determine the leading constant. Review products, powers, signed factors, and substitution in factored expressions. Test 2
Lecture 8Polynomial division and locating rational zeros Connect a zero to a factor, use the remainder and factor theorems, and divide by a linear factor. Demonstrate synthetic division and connect it to polynomial long division. List a small set of Rational Root Theorem candidates from divisors of the constant and leading coefficient, then test sensible candidates. Use examples requiring one division and review missing-term placeholders and signed arithmetic. Test 2
Lecture 9Finding all real polynomial zeros and interpreting signs Combine a short rational-zero search, one division, and a quadratic factor to find all zeros of a cubic or manageable quartic. Include real irrational zeros found with the quadratic formula. Return to the graph to interpret multiplicity, x-intercepts, and positive or negative intervals, including simple polynomial inequalities. Keep factor searches and radical simplification short. Test 2
Lecture 10Rational relations, domain restrictions, and holes Begin with a reciprocal relation and its translated or scaled graphs, then simplify factored rational equations for y. Distinguish a canceled factor's hole from a remaining denominator's vertical asymptote. Find original excluded x-values, hole coordinates, and intercepts. Review numerical fractions and the necessary rational-expression operations, common denominators, and factor cancellation within these tasks. Test 2
Lecture 11Rational graph behavior, asymptotes, and equations Use degree and leading coefficients for horizontal asymptotes, or a short quadratic-by-linear division for a slant asymptote. Describe behavior near a vertical asymptote and at large positive and negative x, and determine range in manageable reciprocal-type examples. Solve short rational equations, including finding x at a specified y, and reject values excluded by original denominators. Test 2
Lecture 12Exponential relations and graphs Develop y = abx with a ≠ 0, b > 0, and b ≠ 1. Distinguish growth, decay, intercept, relative rate, domain, range, and horizontal asymptote. Match several equations to graphs, then shift, reflect, and scale concrete exponential equations. Use exact values at simple inputs, connect fractional and negative powers to earlier exponent laws, and introduce e as a base. Test 3
Lecture 13Logarithmic relations as exponential inverses Reverse an exponential relation to define a logarithm and translate between exponential and logarithmic equations. Evaluate familiar exact logarithms, including common and natural logarithms. Analyze shifted or reflected logarithmic graphs, their domains, ranges, vertical asymptotes, intercepts, and end behavior. Connect their graphs to the corresponding exponentials by reflection across y = x. Test 3
Lecture 14Logarithmic properties and exact evaluation Derive product, quotient, and power properties from exponent laws. Combine or expand short logarithmic expressions while preserving positive-argument conditions. Use the properties to evaluate exact combinations whose separate terms are not immediately evaluable. Introduce change of base with exact values, using powers and simple fractions that work with a four-function calculator. Test 3
Lecture 15Exponential and logarithmic equations Solve exponential equations by rewriting to a common base or, where appropriate, taking logarithms and leaving an exact result. Solve logarithmic equations using a product or quotient property and conversion to exponential form. Include a manageable quadratic result with a rejected candidate. Check positive arguments and revisit linear or quadratic solving within these equations. Test 3
Lecture 16Systems of equations and inequality regions Interpret simultaneous equations as graph intersections and solve by substitution. Begin with a short linear system, then solve a line–parabola or line–circle system with clean intersections. A circle supplies two y-branches for some x-values, so examine the branches separately. Use the same boundaries for a short inequality-region membership check. Review ordered-pair substitution, binomial expansion, and quadratic solving. Test 4
Lecture 17Piecewise relations and graph interpretation Combine explicit familiar equations on specified x-intervals. Evaluate y using the appropriate rule, mark open and closed endpoints, and find domain, range, intercepts, and increasing or decreasing intervals. Read extrema and average change from a supplied graph. Include writing a short piecewise equation from a graph. Teach interval unions and endpoint inclusion where needed. Test 4
Lecture 18General functions and function notation Generalize the function definition introduced in Lecture 2 using the equations, tables, graphs, and mappings already encountered, including numerical and nonnumerical examples. Progress from y = … to y(x), then y1(x) and y2(x), and finally f(x), g(x), and h(x). Evaluate at numbers or expressions. Express familiar graph transformations and inverse relations using general notation, including domain restrictions. Test 4
Lecture 19Function operations, composition, and decomposition Use familiar formulas to add, subtract, multiply, and divide functions, preserving domain restrictions. Form and evaluate compositions, check both the inner and outer domain conditions, and distinguish the order of composition. Recognize inside and outside rules in a given expression, using multiple-choice pairs as well as direct calculations. Verify an inverse by composing in both orders on the appropriate domains. Test 4

Teaching decisions

  • Begin with relations before functions: domain, range, graph transformations, and inverse relations do not require a unique y for each x. Use the diamond and circle first; introduce the unique-output condition in Lecture 2. Revisit intercepts, signs, monotonic intervals, extrema, and end behavior as the concrete function families make each feature meaningful. End behavior can also be discussed for unbounded relations; the opening diamond and circle are bounded.
  • Teach translations and scaling first on the diamond and circle, then extend to equations such as y = (x − 2)2 + 3. The diamond separates horizontal and vertical stretches clearly, but its symmetry hides axis reflections; use a later asymmetric graph to make reflection effects visible. Every relation has an inverse relation obtained by exchanging coordinates. When seeking an inverse function, check uniqueness and restrict the original domain when necessary. These ideas precede the general function-notation templates introduced near the end.
  • The current sequence contains 19 one-hour lectures, leaving one topic to expand later. Absolute value inequalities are excluded from instruction and assessment in this plan. Use review time for fluency in prerequisite factoring, fraction operations, equations, and repeated examples. Keep the core examples short enough to include student attempts within the lecture hour.
  • Lecture 8 supplies the division methods used in polynomial-zero work and slant asymptotes. Keep the polynomial-zero search short, with one division and a remaining quadratic that has real roots. The imaginary unit can be mentioned when negative numerical radicands arise.
  • Test exact algebra and graph interpretation with a basic, non-scientific calculator. Word problems, contextual modeling, regression, and other applications belong in review or extension activities. Difference quotients can extend the earlier average-rate work after function notation is introduced.
  • Use asymmetric supplied graphs when recognizing general transformations in Lecture 18. Earlier graph transformations build the geometric understanding needed for this final notation step.
