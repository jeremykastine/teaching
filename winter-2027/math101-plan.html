Lecture and Assessment Plan

Proposed sequence and test groups · Updated November 10, 2026

Twenty one-hour lectures develop College Algebra in three phases. In Days 1–8, students transform parent graphs and track how points and features move. They read domain and range from graphs and begin with relations before identifying when y is a function of x. Mixed practice is a separate review activity.

In Days 9–16, students solve equations and rewrite expressions to analyze familiar families. For example, changing y = x2 to y = (x − 3)2 − 4 moves the graph. Rewriting (x − 3)2 − 4 as (x − 1)(x − 5) describes the same graph and makes its zeros visible.

In Days 17–20, students introduce general function notation, combine and compose functions, work with inverses, and express the opening graph transformations in general form.

Four proposed unit tests

  1. Graph Transformations (Lectures 1–8). Read and sketch transformations of diamonds, circles, and the parent functions. Use the graph to identify features such as domain, range, vertices, endpoints, and asymptotes.
  2. Quadratics and Polynomials (Lectures 9–12). Rewrite quadratics, solve for zeros, and analyze vertices, extrema, multiplicities, and end behavior.
  3. Rational, Exponential, and Logarithmic Functions (Lectures 13–16). Analyze rational zeros, holes, and asymptotes. Use exponent and logarithm properties to solve equations exactly.
  4. Functions in General (Lectures 17–20). Evaluate, combine, compose, invert, and transform functions using general notation and appropriate domains.

The assessment column gives the proposed test for each lecture's core problem types. Each unit test is designed for 30 minutes and normally has five main questions totaling 100 points.

Days 1–8 · Transform familiar graphs
Lecture 1Transformations of |x| + |y| = 1 and x2 + y2 = 1 Shift and stretch the diamond and circle. Track the center and the four extreme points, then read the domain and range. Review signed coordinates, absolute value, and interval notation as needed. Test 1
Lecture 2Transformations of y = x Shift, stretch, and reflect the line. Describe the resulting slope and intercepts from the graph. Compare lines with the opening diamond and circle to introduce the condition that each allowed x has exactly one y. Compare a right shift of 2 with a downward shift of 2. Test 1
Lecture 3Transformations of y = |x| and y = x2 Apply the same shifts, stretches, and reflections to the V and the parabola. Track the vertex and axis of symmetry, and describe the opening direction, width, and range. Test 1
Lecture 4Transformations of y = √x Track the endpoint and the direction the graph extends. Compare y = √(x − 2) with y = √(2 − x) and use the graphs to see how horizontal and vertical reflections affect domain and range. Test 1
Lecture 5Transformations of y = x3 and y = ∛x Shift, stretch, and reflect both graphs. Track the point that starts at the origin. Swap the coordinates of familiar points to see how reflection across y = x connects the two parent graphs. Test 1
Lecture 6Transformations of y = 1/x and y = 1/x2 Track the vertical and horizontal asymptotes through shifts and compare where the branches lie after stretches and reflections. Read excluded inputs or outputs from the transformed graphs. Test 1
Lecture 7Transformations of y = 2x Track the point that starts at (0, 1) and the horizontal asymptote. Compare axis reflections and observe how a horizontal reflection changes growth into decay. Test 1
Lecture 8Transformations of y = log2 x Begin with the reflection of y = 2x across y = x. Shift, stretch, and reflect the logarithmic graph. Track the vertical asymptote and the point that starts at (1, 0). Test 1
Days 9–16 · Use algebra to explain the graph
Lecture 9Quadratic Forms, Vertices, and Extrema Expand and complete the square to move between standard and vertex form. Find the minimum or maximum and the input where it occurs. Review binomial expansion and signed arithmetic as needed. Test 2
Lecture 10Quadratic Equations and x-Intercepts Set y = 0 and solve by factoring, taking square roots, or using the quadratic formula. Use the discriminant to connect zero, one, or two distinct real solutions to how the graph meets the x-axis. Review factoring, fraction reduction, and square-root simplification as needed. Test 2
Lecture 11Polynomial Zeros and Multiplicity Factor manageable polynomials and find their real zeros. Use each zero’s multiplicity to predict whether the graph crosses or touches the x-axis. Connect the factors in an expression to the x-intercepts of its graph. Test 2
Lecture 12Polynomial End Behavior and Turning Points Use the leading term to predict end behavior. Combine it with zeros and signs between zeros to sketch possible shapes and identify intervals where a local maximum or minimum must occur. Test 2
Lecture 13Rational Functions — Zeros, Holes, and Vertical Asymptotes Factor the numerator and denominator, record excluded inputs, and simplify. Use the remaining factors and the original domain to locate zeros, holes, and vertical asymptotes. Check signs on either side of a vertical asymptote. Test 3
Lecture 14Rational Functions — End Behavior and Asymptotes Compare degrees and leading coefficients to determine whether there is a horizontal asymptote and where it lies. Use a short polynomial division to find a slant asymptote in simple cases. Combine local and end behavior in a sketch. Test 3
Lecture 15Exponential Functions and Equations Isolate the exponential expression. Match bases when possible or use logarithms to write an exact solution. Interpret a solution as the input where the graph reaches a specified height. Review exponent laws where needed. Test 3
Lecture 16Logarithmic Functions and Equations Rewrite between exponential and logarithmic forms. Use the product, quotient, and power properties to solve equations. Check candidate solutions against the positive-argument conditions of the original equation. Test 3
Days 17–20 · Functions in general
Lecture 17Function Notation, Inputs, and Outputs Revisit what makes a relation a function. Move from explicit equations in x and y to y(x), then to f(x) and g(x). Evaluate functions using formulas, graphs, and tables. Distinguish evaluating f(3) from solving f(x) = 3. Test 4
Lecture 18Combining and Composing Functions Build new functions by combining outputs or feeding one output into another function. Track allowed inputs, including the conditions imposed by both rules in a composition. Test 4
Lecture 19Inverse Functions and Restricted Domains Reverse inputs and outputs and connect inverse graphs to reflection across y = x. Verify inverse pairs by composition. Use a restricted quadratic and its square-root inverse to explain how the domain determines which branch is used. Test 4
Lecture 20Transformations of a General Function Use a graph or table for an unspecified f to predict the effects of f(x − h) + k, af(x), and f(bx). Track points, domain, and range using the reasoning from the opening lectures. Test 4

Teaching decisions

  • Use graph transformations throughout Days 1–8. Have students predict the change, track a few key points or features, and read the resulting graph. Begin equation solving on Day 9.
  • The diamond and circle make horizontal and vertical scaling visible. The square-root graph makes the two axis reflections easy to distinguish.
  • Use mixed graph transformation problems as a review activity. Review factoring, fraction operations, and exponent rules where the later lectures need them.
  • Test exact algebra and graph interpretation with a basic, non-scientific calculator. Keep word problems and contextual modeling for review or extension activities.
