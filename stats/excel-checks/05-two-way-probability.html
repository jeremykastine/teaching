Teaching / Excel competency checks

Build a two-way table and compare probabilities

Summarize paired categorical data and choose the correct probability denominators.

Prepare to build and explain a working spreadsheet face to face in about five minutes after practicing. The example below teaches the steps; your instructor may supply new data and choose one of the listed variations. Variations are alternatives, not additional tasks all required in one check.

What to learn

Use formulas to summarize two categorical variables in a contingency table. Calculate an overall probability, a joint probability, and a conditional probability from that table.

Practice example

Put Group and Choice in A1:B1. Paste these tab-separated records into A2:B13:

Morning	A
Morning	A
Morning	A
Morning	A
Morning	B
Morning	B
Afternoon	A
Afternoon	A
Afternoon	B
Afternoon	B
Afternoon	B
Afternoon	B
  1. Set up the table with Group in D1, A in E1, B in F1, and Total in G1. Put Morning in D2, Afternoon in D3, and Total in D4.
  2. In E2 enter =COUNTIFS($A$2:$A$13,$D2,$B$2:$B$13,E$1). Fill across to F2, then down through row 3. Fixed raw ranges and mixed references let one formula count all four combinations.
  3. Use =SUM(E2:F2) in G2 and fill down to G3. Use =SUM(E2:E3) in E4 and fill across through G4. Check the four inner counts are 4, 2, 2, 4 and the grand total is 12.
  4. In a separate labeled area calculate P(A) with =E4/G4, P(Morning and A) with =E2/G4, and P(A given Morning) with =E2/G2. The results are 1/2, 1/3, and 2/3.
  5. Change one Morning record from A to B. Show the table and all three probabilities updating. Explain why the conditional probability uses the Morning total.

(Google Sheets: the formulas are the same. If the pasted records land in one column, select that column and use Data → Split text to columns with the appropriate separator.)

During your check

Build a 2×2 table with totals from a supplied list of about 12 records, calculate the requested probabilities, and demonstrate one changed record. Explain the denominator for each probability.

Variations to prepare for

  • Different labels or list length; adjust the raw-data ranges.
  • The reverse conditional probability, such as P(Morning given A), which uses =E2/E4.
  • A complement, such as P(B given Morning), or comparing P(A given Morning) with P(A) to discuss independence in the observed table. Approximate equality in a small sample does not establish population independence.

Be ready to explain

How “and” differs from “given,” and why switching the condition usually changes the denominator and the result.
