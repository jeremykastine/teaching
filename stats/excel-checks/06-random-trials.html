      Learn the workflow and see a worked example

      What to learn

      Generate random observations efficiently and compare their sample mean with a theoretical expected value. Repeated simulations show how sample size affects the stability of an average.

      Practice example: a fair six-sided die

      1. Put Roll in A1. In A2 enter =RANDBETWEEN(1,6). Fill through A1001. There should be 1,000 simulated rolls. Use the Name Box (the address field beside the formula bar) to select A2:A1001, then Fill Down or Ctrl+D; you do not need to drag through 1,000 rows.
      2. Set up D1:G4 with headings Number of rolls, Observed mean, Expected mean, and Absolute error. Put 10, 100, and 1000 in D2:D4.
      3. In E2 enter =AVERAGE(A2:A11); in E3 enter =AVERAGE(A2:A101); in E4 enter =AVERAGE(A2:A1001). These are nested samples: the first 10, first 100, and all 1,000 from the same experiment.
      4. In F2 enter =(1+2+3+4+5+6)/6, giving the expected mean 3.5. Link F3 and F4 to F2. In G2 enter =ABS(E2-F2) and fill down through G4.
      5. Show at least four decimal places for means and errors. Compare the three errors, then press F9 or use Formulas → Calculate Now several times to generate new experiments in desktop Excel. All three summaries must update together. In Excel for the web, change a spare cell to trigger recalculation.
      6. Explain the pattern across repeated experiments. Larger samples generally have more stable means and are more likely to be close to 3.5. An individual experiment can have a larger error at 100 rolls than at 10 rolls, or at 1,000 than at 100.

      (Google Sheets: use the same formulas and fill-down approach. To trigger a fresh calculation, change a value in an otherwise unused cell. Random functions recalculate as the sheet recalculates; there is no need to use Excel’s F9 shortcut.)

      During your check

      Generate the 1,000 rolls, create the three-row comparison with formula-based means and errors, and explain several recalculations. Your result does not have to get closer at every sample size. Correct construction and interpretation are what you demonstrate.

      Variations to prepare for

      • A fair die numbered 1 through 8 or 1 through 10: change both the generator and the theoretical mean. For equally likely integers 1 through k, the expected mean is =(1+k)/2.
      • Compare the observed proportion of sixes with 1/6 instead of the mean: use, for example, =COUNTIF(A2:A11,6)/COUNT(A2:A11) and corresponding ranges for 100 and 1,000 rolls.
      • Preserve one experiment: copy the generated observations and Paste Special → Values into a separate area. These snapshots stay fixed; live random formulas change on recalculation. In Sheets use Paste special → Values only.

      Optional practice extension

      Build a running mean with =AVERAGE($A$2:A2) in B2 and fill down. Plot it against trial number with a horizontal reference at 3.5. This graph is extra practice, beyond the basic five-minute check.

      Be ready to explain

      The law of large numbers concerns the long-run behavior of an average; it does not guarantee that every added observation improves it or that a particular outcome is “due.” Each die roll still has the same outcome probabilities.
