Set up your spreadsheet

Open a new, blank Excel workbook. Put Observation in A1 and enter your own numbers in A2 downward, one number per cell. Reserve column A for observations only.

One possible summary layout is:

Label cellLabelFormula cellFormula
C2CountD2=COUNT(A:A)
C3MeanD3=AVERAGE(A:A)
C4MedianD4=MEDIAN(A:A)
C5Mode(s)D5=MODE.MULT(A:A)

Keep D6 empty. Enter MODE.MULT once in D5; two modes spill into D5:D6. If you see #SPILL!, clear the cells blocking the results. If you see #N/A, check whether any observation repeats. The text header in A1 is ignored by these formulas.

Changing a number in column A should immediately update the summary. Display enough decimal places to judge the mean error; changing the number format does not change the underlying answer.

Reason about the median

Think about your observations in sorted order. For an odd count, the median is the middle observation. For an even count, it is the average of the two middle observations.

For example, with 24 observations, the median comes from positions 12 and 13. Because the header occupies A1, those positions are A13 and A14 after sorting the observations. Decide what the middle value or pair must be, then consider how many observations should lie on either side. Recheck the middle positions whenever an adjustment changes the order.

Reason about the mode or modes

A mode is a value occurring most often. For a one-mode target, that value must occur more often than every other value. For two modes, both target values must tie for the highest frequency, with every other value occurring less often. Watch for accidental repetitions as you edit. The order in which MODE.MULT lists two modes does not matter.

Reason about the mean

The required total is count × target mean. Changing the total by a small amount changes the mean by that amount divided by the count. If your mean is too low, the total must increase; if it is too high, the total must decrease.

Experiment with values away from the middle positions and required repetitions. Use progressively smaller decimal adjustments while staying between 0 and 1,000. Recheck the count, median, and mode(s) after each adjustment. Your instructor decides whether the mean is close enough; there is no fixed numerical tolerance.

There are many possible datasets. Use these ideas to guide your own guesses and corrections, and be ready to explain how you could narrow the mean error further while preserving the exact requirements.
