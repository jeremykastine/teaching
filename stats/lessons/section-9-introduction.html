Hypothesis testing: conceptual overview

This course studies the ideas and interpretation of hypothesis testing. The sequence follows the textbook’s Sections 9.1–9.5, without the full computational test procedures. A hypothesis test compares sample evidence with what a specified population model would predict.

9.1 · Null and alternative hypotheses

H₀ is the reference claim about a population parameter. Hₐ states the departure being investigated. Choose the direction before looking at the sample result. The equality boundary belongs in H₀; use ≠ for two-sided alternatives and < or > for one-sided alternatives.

Hypotheses about a mean

A service claims a mean completion time of 12 minutes. Write hypotheses to investigate whether the actual mean differs from 12, is greater than 12, or is less than 12. Is the claim about μ or x̄?

  1. Different: H₀: μ=12; Hₐ: μ≠12.
  2. Greater: H₀ at the boundary μ=12 (or composite μ≤12); Hₐ: μ>12.
  3. Less: H₀ at the boundary μ=12 (or composite μ≥12); Hₐ: μ<12.
  4. The hypotheses concern μ, the population mean. The observed sample mean x̄ supplies evidence.

Hypotheses about a proportion

An organization claims that 60% of clients are satisfied. A researcher suspects the true fraction is lower. Write the hypotheses and identify the direction.

H₀: p=0.60 at the reference boundary; Hₐ: p<0.60. This is a left-tailed test. p is the population proportion, while p̂ is the observed sample proportion.

9.2 · Decisions and errors

There are two possible decisions: reject H₀ or fail to reject H₀. A Type I error rejects a true H₀. A Type II error fails to reject a false H₀. The significance level α controls the Type I error rate under the null model. Power is the probability of rejecting H₀ for a specified alternative.

Identify the two errors

A test asks whether the mean service time exceeds 12 minutes. Describe Type I and Type II errors in context.

Alternative is trueCorrect decisionType II error

Type I: conclude evidence of a mean above 12 when the null is true. Type II: fail to detect a mean above 12 when it really is above 12. Failing to reject is not proof that H₀ is true.

Power and study design

How can a larger sample affect power? Does a smaller α automatically make every kind of error less likely?

A larger well-designed sample usually reduces standard error and increases power for a fixed effect size and α. Reducing α makes rejection harder and can increase Type II error if other features stay fixed. Power also depends on effect size and variability. Larger n does not cure bias or dependence.

9.3 · Null distributions and test statistics

The null sampling distribution describes how a statistic varies if H₀ holds. A standardized test statistic measures distance from the null value in standard-error units. The appropriate reference model depends on the parameter, sampling design, known or estimated SD, and distributional conditions.

Read a standardized discrepancy

The null mean is 12, a sample mean is 13.2, and its standard error is 0.4. What does the standardized discrepancy say? Does it alone give a probability?

(13.2−12)/0.4 = 3. The sample mean is three standard errors above the null mean. This is not itself a probability. A reference distribution and a specified tail direction are needed to obtain a p-value.

9.4 · P-values, significance, and conclusions

A p-value is the probability, assuming H₀ and the model assumptions, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one in the direction(s) of Hₐ. It is not the probability that H₀ is true. Compare it with the prespecified α.

Make and state a decision

At α=0.05, one study has p-value 0.018 and another has p-value 0.14. State each decision and a suitable conclusion.

  1. 0.018≤0.05: reject H₀; the sample provides evidence for the stated alternative, subject to the model and design assumptions.
  2. 0.14>0.05: fail to reject H₀; the sample does not provide sufficient evidence for the alternative. This does not establish equivalence or prove H₀.

Interpret a p-value

A report says “p=0.03 means there is a 3% chance that the null hypothesis is true.” Correct it.

If H₀ and the assumptions hold, the probability of evidence at least this extreme, measured by the chosen test statistic and tail(s), is 3%. The p-value does not assign a probability to the truth of H₀.

9.5 · Reading a complete test report

A useful report identifies the population parameter, hypotheses, study design, assumptions, test statistic, p-value, significance level, and contextual conclusion. Also report effect size and uncertainty; statistical significance does not establish practical importance or causation.

Confidence intervals and tests

A 95% confidence interval for a population mean is (12.1,12.9). What would the matching two-sided α=0.05 test of H₀: μ=12 conclude? What qualification is necessary?

Reject H₀, because 12 is outside the interval. This equivalence uses a matching confidence-interval/test procedure with the same assumptions and two-sided level. It should not be applied automatically to mismatched tests or to one-sided questions.

Significance and practical importance

A very large study finds a mean improvement of 0.02 minutes with p=0.001. What can you conclude, and what still matters?

The result may be statistically significant under the model, but 0.02 minute is only 1.2 seconds. Whether that matters depends on costs, context, and uncertainty. Check design, bias, independence, multiple comparisons, and whether causal language is justified.
