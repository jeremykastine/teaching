Data collection experiment

Follow the textbook’s movie-survey sequence: collect observations, select respondents systematically, organize frequencies, and compare groupings. Keep class data separate from the illustrative data below.

1. Collect and select

Each student asks five classmates from another class how many movies they saw at a theater last month. Explain how to select 12 respondents systematically from an ordered class list, then combine their five observations each. What could go wrong?

Select a random start and use a fixed interval chosen to give 12 distinct respondents. The textbook uses a step of four; this works naturally with 48 names. With other list sizes, choose an appropriate interval and check for duplicates rather than repeatedly cycling through the same names. Twelve respondents × five observations = 60 values. Convenience recruitment, repeated people, and ordering patterns can introduce bias; systematic selection at the second stage does not fix first-stage bias.

3. Read percentages

  1. What percent saw at most two movies?
  2. What percent saw at most three?
  3. What percent saw more than two?
  4. What percent saw more than three?
  5. Which table can answer each question exactly, and is one table more correct?
  1. At most two: (5+10+15)/60 = 50%; use the ungrouped table.
  2. At most three: (5+10+15+12)/60 = 70%; either table works.
  3. More than two: 1−0.50 = 50%; use the ungrouped table.
  4. More than three: 1−0.70 = 30%; either table works.
  5. Both tables correctly summarize the same data. Grouping makes patterns easier to see but loses detail within a class; the 2–3 group cannot separate 2 from 3.
