3.4: Contingency Tables

Double-click or double-tap the blank space beneath a problem to show or hide its solution. With a keyboard, focus that space and press Enter or Space.

Reading a contingency table

A contingency table groups observations by two categorical variables. An inside cell counts an intersection (AND). The margins contain row and column totals; the bottom-right entry is the grand total.

For an unconditional probability, divide by the grand total. For P(A | B), read the bar as “given”: restrict attention to B and divide by the total for B. For an OR probability, add the two event totals and subtract their overlap.

Example 3.20 · Drivers and speeding violations

The following fictional study classifies 755 drivers. Choose one driver at random. Let C = uses a cell phone while driving and V = had a speeding violation in the last year.

DriverViolationNo violationTotal
Uses cell phone25280305
Does not use cell phone45405450
Total70685755
  1. Find P(C).
  2. Find P(no violation).
  3. Find P(no violation AND C).
  4. Find P(C OR no violation).
  5. Find P(C | V).
  6. Find P(no violation | does not use cell phone).
  1. P(C) = 305/755 ≈ 0.4040. Use the cell-phone row total.
  2. P(no violation) = 685/755 ≈ 0.9073. Use the no-violation column total.
  3. P(no violation AND C) = 280/755 ≈ 0.3709. Use their common cell.
  4. P(C OR no violation) = (305 + 685 − 280)/755 = 710/755 ≈ 0.9404. Subtract the overlap once.
  5. P(C | V) = 25/70 ≈ 0.3571. The denominator is the 70 drivers with violations.
  6. P(no violation | does not use cell phone) = 405/450 = 0.9. The denominator is the 450 drivers who do not use cell phones.

Missing entries, independence, and conditional probability

Use row and column totals to recover missing entries. To check independence, compare P(A AND B) with P(A)P(B). Equal values mean independent events; unequal values mean dependent events.

Example 3.21 · Hiking preferences

A random sample contains 100 hikers. Let F = woman, M = man, C = prefers the coast, L = prefers lakes/streams, and K = prefers peaks.

HikerCoastLakes / streamsPeaksTotal
Women1816____45
Men________1455
Total____41____100
  1. Complete the missing entries.
  2. Are F and C independent? Compare P(F AND C) and P(F)P(C).
  3. Find P(M | L). What word signals a conditional probability, and which hikers form the restricted sample space?
  4. Find P(F OR K). First identify P(F), P(K), and P(F AND K).
HikerCoastLakes / streamsPeaksTotal
Women18161145
Men16251455
Total344125100
  1. Women who prefer peaks: 45 − 18 − 16 = 11. Men who prefer lakes: 41 − 16 = 25. Men who prefer coast: 55 − 25 − 14 = 16. Add columns for the remaining totals.
  2. P(F AND C) = 18/100 = 0.18. P(F)P(C) = (45/100)(34/100) = 0.153. These differ, so the events are not independent.
  3. “Given” signals the condition. P(M | L) = 25/41 ≈ 0.6098. The restricted sample space is the 41 hikers who prefer lakes/streams.
  4. P(F) = 45/100; P(K) = 25/100; P(F AND K) = 11/100. Thus P(F OR K) = (45 + 25 − 11)/100 = 59/100.

Try It 3.21 · Cycling routes

A random sample of 200 cyclists reports the following route preferences. Let O = older and H = prefers the hilly path.

Age groupLakeHillyWoodedTotal
Younger453827110
Older26521290
Total719039200
  1. Among older cyclists, what is the probability of preferring the hilly path?
  2. Are O and H independent?
  1. P(H | O) = 52/90 = 26/45 ≈ 0.5778.
  2. P(O AND H) = 52/200 = 0.26. P(O)P(H) = (90/200)(90/200) = 0.2025. Unequal, so dependent.

Tables whose entries are probabilities

A probability contingency table uses probabilities instead of counts. All inside cells together sum to 1. A joint entry is P(first event) × P(second event | first event); its factors need not be independent.

Example 3.22 · Three doors

A mouse chooses one of three doors with equal probability. The probabilities of being caught, conditional on choosing Doors 1, 2, and 3, are 1/5, 1/4, and 1/2, respectively.

OutcomeDoor 1Door 2Door 3Total
Caught1/151/121/6____
Not caught4/153/121/6____
Total____________1
  1. Verify the six inside entries and complete every total.
  2. Find P(not caught).
  3. Given that the mouse is caught, find P(Door 1 OR Door 2 | caught).

Each door has probability 1/3. Multiply it by that door’s conditional caught or not-caught probability: (1/3)(1/5) = 1/15, (1/3)(4/5) = 4/15; (1/3)(1/4) = 1/12, (1/3)(3/4) = 3/12; (1/3)(1/2) = 1/6 for each Door 3 outcome.

OutcomeDoor 1Door 2Door 3Total
Caught1/151/121/619/60
Not caught4/153/121/641/60
Total1/31/31/31
  1. Caught total: (4 + 5 + 10)/60 = 19/60. Not-caught total: (16 + 15 + 10)/60 = 41/60. Grand total: 60/60 = 1.
  2. P(not caught) = 41/60 ≈ 0.6833.
  3. P(Door 1 OR Door 2 | caught) = (1/15 + 1/12)/(19/60) = (9/60)/(19/60) = 9/19.

Try It 3.22 · Mutually exclusive purchases

A shopper can buy at most one car. The probability of buying car A is 0.25; the probability of buying car B is 0.65.

  1. Find P(A AND B).
  2. Find P(A OR B).
  1. 0, because buying both is excluded.
  2. 0.25 + 0.65 − 0 = 0.90. The remaining probability, 0.10, is buying neither.
