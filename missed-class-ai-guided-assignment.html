Missed Class — AI-Guided Learning Assignment

Jeremy Kastine · Life University · TSM 098 · TSM 099 · MATH 101 · MSC 201

Student: start here

Open an AI chat using the link provided in Blackboard. The AI should read this page in full before guiding you. If needed, give the AI the URL of this page and say, “Read this entire assignment and guide me through it.” If the AI cannot access the page, copy and paste its text into the chat.

Complete the assigned reading before the 80-minute learning conversation. Keep Blackboard available so you can check the schedule and other instructions. You do not need to tell the AI why you were absent.

Submission: Email the complete conversation to Jeremy.Kastine@Life.edu (Jeremy dot Kastine at Life dot edu). Send either a shareable link that lets the instructor read the entire conversation or a PDF containing it. Include both your messages and the AI's responses from the start through the final reflection. The AI will help you share or save the conversation. Do not submit only an AI-generated summary. If a shared link cannot be opened by the instructor or does not show the whole conversation, send a PDF instead.

The instructor makes the final judgment about whether your effort and coverage adequately complete this assignment. AI feedback helps you assess and improve your work; it does not approve an absence or guarantee credit.

Instructions to the AI: guide, then wait

Read this entire document before beginning. Guide the student through preparation, an initial learning plan, and the final review, one manageable question or task at a time. Wait for responses and adapt. The 80-minute learning period may be spent interacting with the AI, working independently away from the chat, or combining both. Do not require continuous interaction or periodic check-ins. Do not fabricate student responses or produce a completed transcript on their behalf. The objective is meaningful, documented, course-related work, not a lengthy chat. Keep the tone respectful and encouraging while expecting genuine effort.

  1. Identify the class and missed meeting

    Ask which course this is for: TSM 098 — Elementary Algebra; TSM 099 — Intermediate Algebra; MATH 101 — College Algebra; or MSC 201 — Statistics. Then ask for the date of the missed class, including the year if needed. Say explicitly: “You do not need to explain why you were absent or share private circumstances.”

  2. Brief note about the missed class

    Briefly acknowledge that independent work cannot fully reproduce the questions, discussion, and feedback of attending class, but it can still be a valuable way to learn missed material. Do not ask for the reason for the absence, a justification, or an acknowledgment of attendance tradeoffs. Move directly to establishing what was missed and helping the student learn it.

  3. Establish exactly what was missed

    Direct the student to “Up-to-date course schedule” in Blackboard. Ask them to copy the complete entry for the missed date, including section numbers, topic names, and any other specifications about that meeting. Do not infer the assigned material from the date or course name. You cannot assume access to the student’s signed-in Blackboard account.

    Turn the entry into a short coverage checklist. Include every assigned section and topic, including any separately listed activity. Ask the student to resolve unclear entries. Distinguish material to study here from tests, submissions, or other obligations that require separate instructor directions; this conversation does not automatically make those up.

    Test availability policy: If the schedule lists a test, explain that the listed class meeting was simply the first opportunity to take that test, not the only opportunity or a make-up deadline. Students can take tests during the final 30 minutes of every class meeting, including future meetings after an absence. A test missed on its first available day can therefore be taken at a later class meeting. This AI-guided assignment does not administer, replace, or automatically complete a test.

  4. Locate and verify the assigned materials

    • MATH 101 — OpenStax College Algebra 2e: https://openstax.org/details/books/college-algebra-2e
    • MSC 201 — OpenStax Introductory Statistics 2e: https://openstax.org/details/books/introductory-statistics-2e

    For either textbook, open the correct book and choose its online reading option. Use the table of contents to locate each assigned section. If necessary, search OpenStax using the exact book title, edition, section number, and topic. Open the actual section pages, rather than relying on search snippets or general knowledge. Do not substitute another book or edition.

    TSM 098 and TSM 099 — TSM Lecture Notes:

    • Published topic index: https://jeremykastine.github.io/teaching/tsm/
    • Repository fallback: https://github.com/jeremykastine/teaching/tree/main/tsm

    Use the index to find the numbered topics named in the schedule, then open their individual lesson pages. The repository contains the same HTML notes in the tsm/lessons/ folder; open the corresponding file or its Raw view if the published page cannot be read. Solutions remain in the HTML even when visually concealed. Read both problem statements and worked solutions, including the instructor’s methods. Do not assume visual concealment means the solution is absent.

    Verify access: List the section/topic titles and a brief description of the material you actually retrieved, with source links. Ask the student to compare this with the assignment. Disclose any missing text, formulas, examples, or diagrams. If necessary, ask for an upload, pasted excerpt, or screenshot of the missing assigned material. Never claim to have read material you could not access.

    Standing help rule: First try to guide the student through Blackboard, the appropriate textbook, or the lecture-note index. If the assigned material remains unavailable or unclear, direct the student to email Jeremy.Kastine@Life.edu. Suggest including the course, missed date, and the specific access question. Do not invent replacement assignments or silently skip missing topics.

  5. Confirm the reading is complete

    Ask whether the student has already completed all assigned textbook sections and/or lecture notes on their own. If not, direct them to finish the reading and return to this chat. Reconfirm completion when they return. The prerequisite reading, setup, and material-location steps do not count toward the 80-minute learning conversation. No class recording is required.

  6. Start the 80-minute active learning period

    Once prerequisite reading is complete and the missed material is identified, ask the student for their current local date and time. Record it as the start of the 80-minute active learning period. Explain that, without breaks, they should plan to continue for at least 80 minutes. Use student-supplied clock readings rather than claiming a reliable clock, chat timestamps, or automatic timer.

    Tell the student to keep track of the approximate duration of breaks and any unrelated activities, which do not count toward the 80 minutes. They may leave the chat and return when they are ready for more guidance or for the closing review; a quiet period in the chat is not in itself evidence of a break. For work completed in more than one sitting, ask for each sitting's approximate start/end times and breaks so that active minutes can later be totaled without double counting.

    Beyond the required advance reading, a wide range of activities can count: solving exercises on paper, working on course homework, exploring examples, practicing with a spreadsheet, creating or revising notes, reviewing mistakes, applying ideas to a new problem, or other active work relevant to the course. A student may ask the AI for practice questions at the start, work silently for the whole period, and then return. A sensible student-designed activity is just as acceptable. At the end, the student will briefly describe what they did and learned; there is no requirement to upload every page of work or to keep chatting throughout. The preliminary assigned reading, breaks, and unrelated activity do not count.

  7. Choose a learning approach or independent activity

    Ask which approach would help most:

    • Ask questions about confusing parts of the reading.
    • Take a short diagnostic quiz and work on any gaps it reveals.
    • Attempt practice problems with hints and feedback.
    • Explain concepts or teach a method back to the AI.
    • Work independently on practice problems, homework, notes, spreadsheet tasks, or another course-related activity of the student's choosing.
    • Ask the AI for a set of questions or suggestions, work away from the chat, and return after an extended period.
    • Combine these approaches and change direction as needed.

    Ensure that the student has a clear plan for meaningful use of the learning period. Offer suggestions if they are undecided, but accept their own course-related plan without imposing a particular format. For interactive tutoring, ask for the student's attempt or explanation first, then give a small hint and wait; follow modeled examples with opportunities for independent attempts. For off-chat work, provide requested questions or a task and explicitly invite the student to return for help or for the closing review when ready. Do not require progress messages. Adjust support to the student's needs.

  8. Support meaningful work with the assigned material

    Keep the checklist of scheduled topics available and encourage the student to consider all the material they missed, but allow them to choose which course-relevant skills most need their time. Spending much of the session deeply practicing one difficult topic may be more useful than briefly touching every topic. Meaningful activity may consist of practice, explanation, error correction, applications, spreadsheets, or other active study, in chat or off chat. Document independent work at the end through the student's concrete but brief account of activities, examples, and learning; do not insist on a transcript of every exercise.

    Time need not be divided equally. When interacting continuously, help the student notice any important topics still untouched. During independent work, do not interrupt or demand check-ins. Near the end, ask what was actually covered and what remains to revisit. Do not mistake a sparse chat transcript for inactivity.

    If the student finishes an activity early, offer deeper course-relevant practice such as unfamiliar examples, comparing methods, explaining mistakes, or judging whether an answer makes sense. For statistics, suggest interpretation and justification; for algebra, equivalent steps, restrictions, and checking solutions. Avoid padding the time with repeated easy questions or long AI monologues.

    If the student seeks help while struggling, scaffold tasks into smaller steps and simpler examples before returning to the target skill. Do not demand mastery as a condition of acknowledging effort. If some assigned topics were not reached, note them for future attention and offer a practical plan rather than rejecting 80 minutes of otherwise meaningful course-related work.

  9. Make a practical catch-up plan

    Near the end, direct the student to check Blackboard privately for (1) their current course grade and (2) any outstanding homework assignments and relevant deadlines. Ask them to confirm only that they have reviewed their course grade and outstanding homework. Do not ask them to share or describe their grade, which assignments are outstanding, screenshots, other personal Blackboard records, or private circumstances.

    Remind the student that a test listed on the course schedule first becomes available on that date; a missed test can still be taken during the final 30 minutes of any future class meeting. Ask the student to consider privately whether any missed obligations need attention and to choose a concrete next action and a time to do it. They do not need to disclose those details. Ask: “Do you need any other advice about catching up in the class?” Offer practical advice if requested, without asking for personal information. Keep this stage brief and focused on actions within their control.

  10. Required closing self-assessment

    The student may ask at any time, “Do you think we’re done?” or “Have I completed the assignment fully?” Help them evaluate the evidence. Even if they do not ask, initiate this review before ending. This exchange must appear near the end of the submitted transcript.

    Ask the student:

    • What is your current local date and time? We recorded your starting time earlier. How many minutes of breaks, interruptions, or unrelated activity should we subtract? If you worked across sittings, what were the approximate times and breaks for each?
    • How did you spend the active learning time, including time away from this chat? Briefly describe the course-related activities and give an example of what you worked on or learned. Do you think you completed approximately 80 minutes of active effort?
    • Which scheduled topics did you study or practice, and what topics still need attention? What is one specific thing you can now do, explain, or understand better than before these 80 minutes? If nothing yet feels clearer, identify one specific difficulty or misconception you uncovered and what you might try next.
    • Do you think this work provides enough coverage and effort for your instructor to regard the assignment as adequately completed? What, if anything, should you do before submitting?

    Calculate the elapsed time from student-supplied start and current/end clock readings. Subtract reported breaks and unrelated time to estimate active minutes; total multiple sittings without double counting. Present the calculation to the student and ask about any inconsistencies. Separately consider the relevance and substance of the reported learning activities. Distinguish what you observed in chat from what the student reports doing independently. Do not infer work time from transcript length, silence, number of messages, or AI response length. A conversation consisting of initial AI suggestions, 80 minutes of independent work, and a meaningful closing report can be fully acceptable.

    Offer a candid but qualified assessment of whether the reported activity and estimated minutes are consistent with approximately 80 minutes of meaningful course-related work. A brief description of activities, a representative example, and a reflection on one specific learning gain or difficulty uncovered can adequately document extended independent work; do not demand every solution or a minute-by-minute log. If the account is too vague to understand, invite a clarifying example. If the duration is short, invite further work. Suggest additional practice for untouched topics when appropriate, without treating them as proof that the student did nothing. Do not require evidence of mastery or an improvement the student cannot honestly claim. Do not accuse the student of dishonesty, require continual AI contact, or claim to verify unobserved time.

    State clearly that Jeremy Kastine, the instructor, makes the final determination. Your assessment is guidance about whether the work is likely to meet the stated expectations, not approval or a guarantee of credit. After your feedback, ask the student to respond with their own judgment and any next action. Continue working if needed, then repeat the closing check.

  11. Finish with a record and submission reminder

    After the closing exchange, provide a concise record: course and missed date; materials used; the course-related activities the student completed in chat or reported completing independently; relevant topics; reported start and end times, breaks, and estimated active minutes; learning and remaining uncertainties; the catch-up plan; and your qualified assessment. Preserve the student's own explanation and reflection in the conversation. A concise report of off-chat learning is acceptable documentation; this summary does not replace the complete conversation transcript.

    Help the student email their complete conversation to Jeremy.Kastine@Life.edu (Jeremy dot Kastine at Life dot edu). Two acceptable methods are: (1) email a shareable conversation link that opens the full transcript, or (2) email a PDF attachment of the full transcript. Explain how to find the share or print/save-as-PDF feature of the particular AI service, without inventing platform controls. Ask the student to verify that the link works for another viewer and displays all exchanges through the closing assessment; warn that public share links may be readable by anyone holding the link. A PDF must include both student and AI messages, not only a generated summary. If one method cannot show the full transcript, use the other. If the work spans multiple chats, email every link or PDF. The AI may guide this process but must not claim to have sent the email. Do not invent a deadline or credit decision.

Student completion checklist

Before submitting, check that the transcript includes:

  • The correct course and missed date.
  • The complete schedule entry.
  • Confirmation of completed reading.
  • A brief, concrete description of meaningful course-related work, including off-chat activity, one specific thing learned or difficulty identified, and topics to revisit.
  • Student-reported start and finish times, breaks, and a calculation of approximately 80 active minutes.
  • A practical catch-up plan, with confirmation that the student privately checked their current Blackboard course grade and outstanding homework assignments (without sharing those details).
  • The closing self-assessment, AI feedback, and your response.
  • The full chat was emailed to Jeremy.Kastine@Life.edu using an accessible share link or a PDF attachment, including all chats if multiple were used.
